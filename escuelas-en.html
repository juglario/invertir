Schools of thought

There is no single way to invest.

Six schools, side by side. Each one has its big names, a central idea, a cost and critics. We do not pick one for you: we show you what you would have to believe for each one to work.

How to read this

Six serious paths. None of them is free.

Famous investors do not agree with each other, and that is not a flaw in investing: it is a sign that each path costs something different. One asks for patience, another asks for hours of study, another asks you to put up with looking wrong for years. That is why each school comes with the same four questions: what you have to believe, where it fails, who argues with it and who it fits.

If you are just starting, the index school is the one the Getting Started guide recommends, because it asks for less time and less luck. But knowing the others helps you understand what is behind what you read, hear and get sold.

The schools

Six ideas, six costs.

School · Big names: John Bogle

You do not need to beat the market: it is enough not to lose to it

Buy the whole market, pay as little as possible in fees and let time do the rest.

John Bogle founded Vanguard in 1975 and the next year launched the first index fund for ordinary people. An index fund does not pick stocks: it copies an index, which is a list of companies, like the S&P 500, which groups about 500 of the biggest companies in the United States. On Wall Street they laughed at it and nicknamed it "Bogle's folly". Today it is one of the most popular ways to invest in the world.

The idea rests on simple math. All investors together are the market, so on average they earn what the market earns, before costs. After paying fees, the one who tries to pick ends up, on average, behind. The SPIVA report, published by S&P Dow Jones Indices, shows that over 15-year periods about 9 out of 10 active funds (the ones that pick stocks) investing in large US companies earned less than their index. And costs compound against you: $10,000 at 7% a year for 40 years turns into about $150,000; at 6%, meaning the same return minus a 1% fee, about $103,000. That one tiny point eats almost a third.

The most famous example came from someone in another school. In 2007 Warren Buffett bet a million dollars, to go to charity, that an S&P 500 index fund would beat, over ten years, a group of hedge funds (expensive funds run by professionals) chosen by a rival. From 2008 to 2016 the index earned about 7.1% a year and the chosen funds, on average, about 2.2%, according to Buffett's 2017 letter. By the end of the bet, in 2017, the index had gained 125.8% and the funds 36.3%.

For it to work you have to believe that market prices do a pretty good job of reflecting what is known, that the economy you invest in will grow over the long term, and that you will be able to sit still when everything falls.

Where it fails In the S&P 500 each company counts according to what it is worth on the stock market, so if one goes up a lot, the fund owns more of it right when it is most expensive: in 2025 the ten biggest already made up more than a third of the index. A single-country index can take decades to recover: Japan's Nikkei hit its peak in 1989 and only passed it in 2024, not counting dividends. And by definition you never beat the market: you are the market minus a tiny cost.

Who argues with it The value school says that buying without looking at the price means giving up on thinking. The concentration school believes that spreading across 500 companies waters down your best ideas. The trend school asks why you would sit through a 50% drop if you could have gotten out earlier. And other critics ask who will set the right prices if everyone buys the index without looking.

It fits if you want to invest without spending hours on it, you do not mind boring, and you have the patience to look rarely and touch nothing for years.

Try it: look up two funds that copy the S&P 500 and write down their yearly cost (the expense ratio). With a compound interest calculator, compare $1,000 over 40 years at 7% minus each of those costs, and then against a fund that charges 1%. Write down how much money gets lost along the way.

School · Big names: Benjamin Graham, Warren Buffett, Charlie Munger

The price you pay decides what you earn

Buy pieces of good businesses only when they cost less than they are worth, and wait patiently for the market to notice.

Benjamin Graham wrote the founding books of this school, Security Analysis (1934) and The Intelligent Investor (1949). His central idea: a stock is not a slip of paper that goes up and down, it is a piece of a business. If you can estimate what that business is worth, you wait until the price is well below it. That gap is the margin of safety.

Warren Buffett, Graham's student, started out buying dirt-cheap stocks of mediocre companies, which Graham compared to cigar butts thrown in the street: they had one last free puff left. His partner Charlie Munger convinced him of something else: a wonderful company at a fair price is better than a fair company at a wonderful price. The classic example is See's Candies, a chocolate maker in California. In 1972 Berkshire Hathaway, Buffett's company, paid 25 million dollars, about three times its book value (what its accounting records say it has). For Graham that was expensive. But See's could raise its prices every year without losing customers and barely needed to invest to keep running. By 2007 it had already brought in about 1,350 million dollars in pre-tax profit, according to Buffett's letter from that year.

Tools from this school. Charlie Bilello, a market analyst, builds three scenarios (bad, normal and good) and asks: at this price, how much do earnings have to grow for the stock to return, for example, 8% to 10% a year? If it only works in the good scenario, the price already assumes everything will go perfectly. And he warns that valuation says a lot about the next ten years and almost nothing about next year. Sven Carlin, an investor who teaches on YouTube, places each stock on a two-axis grid: how much it could return per year over the next decade, and how likely it is to lose money for good (because of debt, a business that depends on cycles, or a company that keeps issuing new shares nonstop). He looks for the low-risk, high-return corner. His rule: if the price goes up a lot and the business did not change, the stock moves on the grid, because now it promises to return less.

For it to work you have to believe that the market sometimes overreacts out of fear or euphoria and corrects over time, that within what you understand well (Buffett calls it your circle of competence) you can estimate value better than average, and that you will put up with years of losing to the index without giving up.

Where it fails In the value trap: something cheap that stays cheap because the business is dying. Also, the style can go a long time without working: in the US, cheap stocks fell behind for much of 2007 to 2020 (you can see it in Factors). And even Buffett gets it wrong: he bought airline stocks and sold them at a loss in 2020, when the pandemic broke his idea.

Who argues with it The index school answers that almost nobody beats the market after costs, and points out that Buffett himself recommended an index fund for most people. The trend school says that what is cheap can keep falling and that it is better to buy what is already going up.

It fits if you like reading financial statements and doing the math, you are patient, and you can go against the crowd without stressing out when everyone is buying what you are not.

Try it: pick a company whose products you use. Write in three lines how it makes money and why a competitor would have a hard time copying it. Then look up its P/E (what it is) and build Bilello's three scenarios by hand: if its earnings grow 0%, 5% or 10% a year for five years, how much would you earn in each case? In which of the three does everything have to go right for it to be worth it to you?

School · Big names: Howard Marks

You do not need to guess, it is enough to know where you are

The market swings back and forth between euphoria and panic, and this school adjusts how much risk it takes depending on where it thinks the pendulum is.

Howard Marks is a cofounder of Oaktree Capital, a firm that specializes in buying the debt of companies in trouble. Since 1990 he has written letters to his clients (the famous "memos"), read by people like Warren Buffett. You already saw the underlying idea in The market pendulum: psychology pushes prices to extremes. What this school adds is what to do about it. Marks does not try to predict, meaning he does not say "next year the market will fall". He makes a diagnosis, like a doctor who does not know when you will get sick but can see whether you have a fever today.

In practice, it means turning a dial. When there is a lot of optimism, little fear and easy credit, the cycles investor gets more defensive: buys less, demands lower prices and keeps more cash. When there is panic and nobody wants to lend, they get more aggressive and use that cash. For Marks, risk is not the price going up and down, but losing money for good (you saw it in Risk is not the price moving), and that risk is highest exactly when everyone feels safe. Oaktree's motto sums it up: "if we avoid the losers, the winners will take care of themselves".

A real example: on January 2, 2000, in the middle of the internet craze, Marks published a memo called "bubble.com" warning that the prices of many tech companies made no sense. Months later the bubble started to deflate. And in 2008, in the middle of the crisis, when almost nobody wanted to take risks, Oaktree raised about 11 billion dollars between 2007 and May 2008 to buy debt from companies that had been hit hard.

For it to work you have to believe that people's psychology repeats itself, that an excess can be recognized by watching signals (how much celebrating there is, how easy it is to borrow, how many companies with no profits go public) and that you will have patience and cash set aside for when the panic arrives.

Where it fails A diagnosis is not a date. Marks often says that being too early looks a lot like being wrong: if you turn defensive in the third year of a rally that lasts seven, you watch everyone make money while you wait. Also, the signals are not exact numbers, and two honest people can look at the same thing and read different things. And without cash set aside, panic is an opportunity you can only watch.

Who argues with it The index school: nobody, even with good signals, knows when a rally ends, so it is better to stay invested and not touch any dial. The antifragile school: saying "we are near the top" is already a kind of prediction; better to build something that holds up no matter what moment we are in.

It fits if you are patient, you can handle going against what everyone else is doing (buying when others sell in fear, holding back when everyone is celebrating) and you would rather read, observe and take notes than trade often.

Try it: answer Marks's three questions using this week's news: how much optimism is there? How afraid of losing are people? How easy is it to borrow money? Give each one a score from 1 (panic) to 5 (euphoria) and write down why. Keep the sheet and repeat it in three months. The exercise is not about being right, it is about seeing whether your read changes and why.

School · Big names: Nassim Nicholas Taleb

Do not predict: set yourself up to survive being wrong

Because extremely rare events happen more often than we think, this school puts almost all the money somewhere safe and a small part in bets where you can lose a little and win a lot.

Nassim Taleb was an options trader (contracts that work a bit like insurance) for about 20 years and wrote books like The Black Swan (2007) and Antifragile (2012). He splits things into three types: the fragile breaks when hit (a mug), the robust takes the hits (a rock) and the antifragile gets better from them (your muscles when you train). His starting point is that in markets, extremes are not that rare: big crashes happen a lot more often than the bell-shaped curve that many models use says they should. He calls this fat tails, because the ends of the curve, the "tails", weigh more than they seem to.

To explain it he uses the turkey problem. A turkey gets fed every day for 1,000 days, and each day it is more sure that the farmer loves it. On day 1,001, shortly before Thanksgiving, the farmer does not come with food. The turkey's confidence was highest exactly when the danger was highest. Many calm days do not prove that something is safe.

His answer is the barbell (the gym bar, with weight on both ends and nothing in the middle). On one end goes most of the money (Taleb talks about 85 to 90%) in very safe things. On the other goes a small part (10 to 15%) in bets where the worst case is losing what you put in, but the best case can be many times that. In the middle there is none of the "sort of risky" stuff, which, according to him, feels safe without being safe. It is First, survive turned into a way of building everything. A real example: in March 2020, when the S&P 500 fell about a third in about five weeks, Universa, a fund that had Taleb as an advisor and that buys insurance against crashes, reported huge gains on that slice. Careful: those are the fund's own numbers, not audited, and other investors argue about whether that strategy pays off over the long run.

For it to work you have to believe that what matters about the future cannot be predicted, that big shocks will come even if you do not know when, and that it is worth earning less in the calm years in exchange for never getting knocked out of the game.

Where it fails The safe end is not that safe: with high inflation or devaluations, something "safe" in one currency can lose buying power in another. The small bets tend to lose a little almost all the time (Taleb calls it "bleeding"), and that wears you down. The 90/10 split is also an opinion, it does not come from any formula. And buying insurance against crashes costs money: studies by the asset manager AQR argue that over the long run it usually costs more than it pays.

Who argues with it The concentration school: knowing a business inside out is a real edge and not a fairy tale, and putting a lot into a few good ideas is how many fortunes were made. The index school: buying the whole market and waiting paid off well for decades without paying for insurance. The cycles school agrees about avoiding losers, but believes you can read what moment you are in.

It fits if thinking about what could go wrong keeps you up at night, you would rather lose a little often than a lot all at once, and you can handle others seeming to earn more than you for years.

Try it: build an imaginary barbell with $10,000 of pretend money. Write down where you would put $9,000 so it is as safe as possible, and what risks it still has (inflation, currency). Then think of a $1,000 bet where the worst case is losing that $1,000 (it can be something outside the market, like a course or a project of your own). Write down how much you have left if everything goes wrong. If the number scares you, the safe end was not that safe.

School · Big names: Mohnish Pabrai, Warren Buffett, Charlie Munger

A few bets, well chosen

If almost all wealth is created by a few companies, this school tries to find them and bet big on them instead of spreading out across a hundred.

The starting point is a fact. Hendrik Bessembinder, a finance professor, studied about 26,000 US stocks from 1926 to 2016 and published the result in 2018: around 4% of those stocks account for all the wealth the stock market created above Treasury bills (very short loans to the US government, with almost no risk). About 4 out of every 7 stocks earned less than those bills over their whole life. Most stocks are not worth it. A few are worth a huge amount.

That is where Buffett and Munger's idea of making a few big bets comes from. Buffett suggested imagining a card with 20 punches: every investment you make in your life uses one up. With only 20, you would only buy what seems obvious to you. Mohnish Pabrai, an investor who has run funds since the late nineties, adds two tools. The first is cloning: instead of inventing ideas, look at what investors you admire are buying and then study those companies yourself. The second is a checklist built from other investors' famous mistakes, which he goes over before every purchase.

An example of cloning: in August 2020, Berkshire Hathaway, Buffett's company, announced that it owned about 5% of each of five big Japanese trading houses. That information was public from day one. Cloning is not copying the purchase blindly: it is using that announcement as the starting point for your own research.

For it to work you have to believe that you can spot the 4% before everyone else, or copy someone who can, and that you will hold on without selling when your bet falls 40% and everyone tells you that you got it wrong.

Where it fails Bessembinder's data looks backward: today it is easy to know who the winners were, but at the time it was not. If you pick a few stocks at random, the most likely outcome is that none of them is in the 4%. Also, we see the concentrated investors who won, not the many who went broke: that is called survivorship bias. According to S&P's SPIVA reports, most funds that pick stocks earn less than their index over long periods.

Who argues with it The index school answers that, if nobody knows which ones are the 4%, it is better to buy the whole market and be sure to own them. The antifragile school warns that one huge bet that goes wrong can knock you out of the game. To tell conviction apart from fondness for a brand, see Knowing a company is not having an edge.

It fits if you like reading financial statements for hours, you can stand spending years looking wrong, and you can say no to almost everything.

Try it: imagine you have 20 punches left on your card. Pick a company and write in five lines why you would use one on it. Then write down three things that, if they happened, would make you admit you were wrong.

School · Big names: Narasimhan Jegadeesh and Sheridan Titman, David Galán

Follow the price, not the story

What has been going up tends to keep going up for a while, so this school jumps on the trend and jumps off when it breaks.

Momentum is the idea that the stocks that went up the most over the last 3 to 12 months tend to keep doing better than the rest for a while. In 1993, professors Jegadeesh and Titman measured it with US stocks from 1965 to 1989: buying the ones that had gone up the most and selling the ones that had gone down the most made money over the following months. Many later studies found something similar in other countries and markets. If you want to see momentum as a factor, it is in Factors.

This school does not ask what a company is worth. It asks where the price is heading. A classic trend-following rule uses the 200-day moving average, which is the average price over the last 200 market days. If the price is above that average, you stay in. If it falls below, you get out. The Spanish educator David Galán adds a chart-based view: when a stock goes past all its previous highs, it is in "free rise", because there are no old buyers waiting to sell to get back what they lost.

A real example: the S&P 500 fell about 57% between October 2007 and March 2009. That rule took you out of the market in late 2007 or early 2008, before most of the fall. But it only got you back in around mid-2009, after a strong bounce off the bottom.

For it to work you have to believe that people react late to news and catch it from each other, so prices move in waves and not all at once.

Where it fails When the trend flips suddenly. In 2009, the strategy of buying winners and selling losers between March and May 2009 the past losers rose more than 150% while the past winners gained only about 8%, according to Daniel and Moskowitz (2016),, because the hardest-hit stocks bounced back hard. In markets that move sideways, the average rule makes you get in and out many times on false signals, and every move costs fees and taxes.

Who argues with it The value school says that buying what already went up means paying too much, and that price without value is smoke. The index school answers that all that trading just hands costs to the broker.

It fits if you like clear rules more than opinions, you can sell without fighting the price, and you accept many small losses in exchange for avoiding a few big ones.

Try it: look up a free chart of the S&P 500 with the 200-day average. Count how many times the price crossed it in the last five years and write down what would have happened if you had followed the rule each time.
