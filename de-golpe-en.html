For when money arrives all at once

You came into a fortune. Now comes the hard part.

You sold your company, signed the contract of your life, your song blew up, you got a huge bonus or you received an inheritance. The money arrived. Whether it stays depends on what you do in the next few months.

What the data shows

More money doesn't solve the problem. It postpones it.

What you think "I'm set"

With this money I'm set for life.

What they measured 3 to 5 years

Florida lottery winners of between 50,000 and 150,000 dollars: in the first years they filed for bankruptcy less often than those who won small amounts, but between the third and fifth year the difference disappeared. The prize postponed bankruptcy. It did not prevent it.

It is not just the lottery. A study of American football players found the same thing by another route: those who earned more money and played more years did not go bankrupt less often than the rest. The amount does not protect you. What protects you is what you do with it.

Sources: Hankins, Hoekstra and Skiba, "The Ticket to Easy Street? The Financial Consequences of Winning the Lottery", Review of Economics and Statistics, 2011. Carlson, Kim, Lusardi and Camerer, American Economic Review, 2015.

Why it happens

Big money fools the brain.

1

The mental math of big money.

When a huge sum arrives, the brain feels it as endless: "with a million, what's 50,000 for a car?" But every expense eats a piece of the capital that should be working for you your whole life. The mistake is not one purchase. It is letting your lifestyle settle around money that does not refill itself.

2

The pause rule.

Before doing anything big, wait. Six months with the money in something safe and liquid costs you almost nothing, and it protects you from the worst moment to decide: the moment of euphoria. Decisions that cannot be undone, like buying a house, putting money into a business or lending, can wait.

3

Don't keep everything in the thing that made you rich.

If you are paid in stock of the company you work for, or you sold your company in exchange for stock in another one, your salary and your savings are tied to the same thing. When Enron collapsed in 2001, many employees lost their jobs and their savings at the same time, because much of their retirement plan was in Enron stock. Diversifying here is not distrust. It is not betting twice on the same card.

4

Your freedom number.

The question that matters is not how much you have, but how much you can spend each year without it running out. A classic rule says to withdraw between 3 and 4% a year. With a million, that is between 30,000 and 40,000 a year. If you want to spend 80,000, you need twice as much. Do the math before deciding your new lifestyle, not after.
