Price, value and the margin of safety.

The price is what you pay and the value is what you get. The margin of safety is the gap between them, and it protects you on the day you are wrong.

Two numbers people confuse

You see the price. You have to estimate the value.

The price is what the market asks today. You see it on a screen, it changes every day, and you do not have to think to know it. The value is something else: what something is really worth, based on the money it will generate over its life. A share is a small piece of a company (if you need that base, see lesson 5), and its value depends on how much that company will earn over many years. No screen shows that. You have to estimate it.

In his 2008 letter to shareholders, Warren Buffett wrote a line he credited to his teacher, Benjamin Graham: price is what you pay, value is what you get. The two usually stay close. But fear or excitement can pull them far apart, and that distance is the whole idea of this entry.

Think of something you know. A used phone can be listed for $300 in one place and $180 in another, for the same model. The phone did not change. The seller's hurry or mood did. Companies work the same way, except the "phone" is a whole business and nobody knows its exact value.

Graham's idea

Since nobody knows the exact value, buy with a cushion.

Graham wrote The Intelligent Investor in 1949 and devoted its final chapter to one idea, which he called the central concept of investment: the margin of safety. It means buying only when the price is well below what you believe the thing is worth. The arithmetic is simple:

margin of safety = (estimated value − price) / estimated value

If you think something is worth $100 and you pay $60, your margin is 40%. What does it do? It protects you on the day you were wrong. If it was really worth $80, you still paid less than it is worth. If you were right and the price reaches $100, you made $40 on $60, about 67%. Both good outcomes come from the price you paid.

Buffett explained it with a bridge, in a 1984 talk: when you build a bridge, you insist it can carry 30,000 pounds, but you only drive 10,000-pound trucks across it. You do not design the bridge to carry exactly what will cross. You build in spare capacity because you do not know everything. Investing with a margin works the same way: you leave room for what you did not see.

Try it

Move the price and see what happens if you were wrong.

Suppose you estimated that something is worth $100. Choose what you pay and how wrong you could be. The simulator builds three scenarios: the real value is lower than you thought, you were right, or you were too cautious. For each one it calculates what you would gain or lose if, over time, the price ends up at the real value.

Look at the red line, the bad scenario. While the price you pay is below it, being wrong does not make you lose. That is the job of the margin: it does not make you gain more when you are right (a low price does that in general), it protects you when you are not. And if you could be very wrong, you need to pay much less to be covered.

The partner with moods

Mr. Market offers you a price every day. You are not obliged to take it.

In chapter 8 of the same book, Graham invented a character: Mr. Market. Imagine you co-own a business with a partner who knocks on your door every day and offers to buy your share or sell you his, at a price that changes with his mood. Some days he is euphoric and asks for too much. Other days he is depressed and nearly gives it away. The business is the same both days.

Graham's point is that you do not have to listen to him. His price is an offer, not an order. If he is euphoric, you let it go. If he is depressed and the price falls well below value, you take advantage. Buffett wrote that chapters 8 and 20 of that book were the foundation of how he invests.

The simulator below invents a Mr. Market. The business value grows slowly, 4% a year. The price jumps around that value depending on the day's mood. You choose two things: how moody he is and how much margin you demand before buying.

The chart holds two lessons. First: with a high margin, every purchase is at a good price, because you only bought when Mr. Market was depressed. Second, less pleasant: with a very high margin, sometimes years go by without a single offer. The margin is not free. You pay for it with patience, and sometimes with opportunities you miss.

How value gets estimated

You do not need an exact number. You need an honest range.

The obvious question is: how do I know what something is worth? The short answer is that nobody knows exactly, which is why the margin exists. The long answer is about how much money the business will generate in the future, and you will see it in more detail in the entry on how a company is valued. To start, three ideas are enough:

  • Think in ranges, not one number. Instead of "worth $100", say "worth between $75 and $125". If you cannot give a range, you do not understand the business well enough yet.
  • Decide using the low end of the range. If the price is below the most pessimistic value you consider reasonable, you have a real margin. If it is only cheap against your optimistic scenario, you have no margin: you have hope.
  • The less you know, the more margin you demand. A large, stable company that has sold the same kind of thing for decades can be estimated within a narrower range than a new company that does not yet earn money. For the second, a 20% margin may be too little.

Common mistakes

Cheap does not automatically mean you have a margin.

The margin of safety is a simple idea that is often misused. These are the mistakes that come up most:

  • Confusing "it fell a lot" with "it is cheap". A share that fell 60% tells you nothing about its value. Sometimes the business broke and the new price is fair. The margin is measured against value, not against the old price.
  • Inflating the value so the margin appears. When you want to buy something, it is easy to convince yourself it is worth more. Write your estimate before you look at the price, not after.
  • Believing a great company does not need a margin. An excellent company bought at a very high price can be a bad investment. Quality does not give you the margin; the price does. This connects to Risk is not the price moving: overpaying is one of the most common ways to suffer a permanent loss.
  • Waiting forever for the perfect margin. As the simulator showed, if you demand too much, you may never buy. For most people, the simplest way not to overpay is to buy the whole market through an index fund, gradually and over many years, instead of hunting bargains one by one.

The bigger idea

The margin of safety is not just a price. It is a way of building everything.

Once you understand the idea, you see it everywhere. An emergency cushion is a margin of safety: if you lose your job, you do not have to sell at the worst moment. Not putting everything into one thing is a margin of safety: if one goes wrong, it does not drag everything down. Not investing with borrowed money is a margin of safety: nobody can force you to sell. The entry Survival first takes this idea to its end.

The underlying question is always the same: what happens if I am wrong? If the answer is "I lose a bit and carry on", you have a margin. If the answer is "I lose everything", it does not matter how sure you feel.

To do

Set a maximum price before you look at the price.

Try it: think of something used that you know well, like a phone, a bike or a console. Write three numbers on paper: its minimum value, its most likely value and its maximum value. Then decide your maximum purchase price, for example the minimum value minus 10%. Only then look for five listings. For each one, calculate the margin against your most likely value: (value minus price) divided by value. How many pass your rule? If none pass, that is also an answer: today Mr. Market is not giving anything away.
