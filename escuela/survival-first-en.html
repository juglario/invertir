Survival first.

Before chasing big gains, the first rule is to stay in the game. Starting from zero, compound interest has nothing to work with.

The word that matters

Some losses hurt. Others end the game.

Nassim Taleb spent years as a market practitioner and then wrote several books about chance and rare events. One idea runs through all of them: not all losses are equal. Some recover with time. Others take you out of the game. The second kind is called ruin.

Ruin is not only losing all your money. It is losing so much that you can no longer continue: selling what you had left, stopping investing for years, or changing your life to pay a debt. If that happens, it does not matter how well things went before. Compound interest multiplies what you have, and any number multiplied by zero is zero.

Taleb often uses a deliberately horrible example: Russian roulette. Someone offers you a lot of money to pull a revolver trigger once with one bullet in six chambers. Five times out of six you win. If you look only at the average, the game looks worth it. But nobody sensible would play, because on the sixth try there is no second chance. Investing is not that extreme, but the logic is the same: a strategy that works almost always but occasionally ruins you will, over time, ruin you.

The cruel arithmetic

Losing and gaining the same percentage does not leave you even.

If you have $100 and lose 50%, you are left with $50. To get back to $100 you need to gain $50 on $50, which is +100%. If you lose 20%, +25% is enough ($20 on $80). If you lose 90%, you are left with $10 and need +900%. If you lose 100%, no gain can save you.

gain needed = loss / (1 − loss)

That is why a 50% rise followed by a 50% fall does not leave you where you started: $100 becomes $150 and then $75. You lost a quarter. The order does not matter: falling 50% then rising 50% also leaves you at $75. Big losses weigh more than gains of the same size.

The dotted line is what it would look like in a fair world: lose 30% and gain back 30%. The real curve pulls away more and more. Up to 10% or 20% the difference barely shows. Past 50% it shoots up. The practical conclusion is that avoiding very large losses is worth more than chasing very large gains. The same arithmetic appears in Risk is not the price moving, from the side of how long recovery takes.

Being right is not enough

Even with an edge, betting too much can ruin you.

Here is the surprising part. Imagine a game that favours you: a coin that lands your way 60 times out of 100. When it does, you win what you bet. When it does not, you lose it. You have an edge. How much of your money would you bet on each flip?

Intuition says "a lot, if I have an edge". The mathematics says something else. In 1956, a researcher at Bell Labs, John Kelly, published a formula for this question. For a bet like this, the share of your money that grows it most in the long run is:

Kelly bet = 2 × probability of winning − 1

With a 60% chance, that gives 20%. If you bet more than that, you grow less. If you bet much more, the typical player ends up losing, even with an edge on every flip. Try it: keep the probability at 60% and raise the bet.

Why does this happen? The same asymmetry as before. Betting 50%, a won flip takes you from $100 to $150, and a lost one from $150 to $75. Winning one and losing the next leaves you worse than you started. Since you will win some and lose others, each "win then lose" pair eats money, and a 60% edge is not enough to compensate.

There is one more detail worth noticing in the simulation. With large bets, the average of 1,000 players can be very high, because a few lucky ones end with fortunes. But the median, which is what happens to the player in the middle, is miserable. You will not be the average of a thousand people: you will live one of those stories. That is why it helps to look at the typical player, not the average.

Where ruin comes from

Almost always through the same three paths.

Ruin rarely comes from a lightning strike. It usually arrives through a handful of decisions made earlier, when things were going well:

  • Putting everything in one thing. If all your money depends on one company, one currency or one business, a problem there is a problem everywhere. It is the investing version of betting 100% on every flip. Lesson 8 explains how to spread risk.
  • Investing with borrowed money. If you borrow to invest, a fall you could have survived becomes one you cannot: the lender asks for the money exactly when the price is low, and you have to sell at the worst moment. Debt turns volatility into permanent loss.
  • Having no cushion. Without savings for emergencies, any unexpected expense forces you to sell whatever you hold, at whatever price there is. The cushion in lesson 2 is, literally, insurance against ruin.

None of the three depends on picking the right thing to buy. You can have the best idea in the world and still be ruined if you bet too much, with debt and no cushion. The other way round, an ordinary idea at a prudent size lets you keep playing for a long time.

One way to build it

The gym barbell: very safe on one side, a little risk on the other.

In Antifragile, Taleb proposes what he calls the barbell strategy, after the gym weight bar: two heavy ends and nothing in the middle. A large share of your money goes into very safe things that cannot ruin you. A small share goes into high-potential bets, knowing you could lose all of it. What you avoid is the confusing middle: things that look safe but hide a large risk.

An example with round numbers, as an illustration and not a recipe: if you put 90% in something very safe and 10% in something very risky, the worst outcome is losing about 10% of the total. That hurts, but it recovers with about +11%. The best outcome has no ceiling. Your loss has a limit you chose; your gain does not.

You do not need this exact shape. The useful question is, for each part of your money: what is the worst that can happen here, and can I live with it? If the worst case is something you cannot survive, the size is wrong, no matter how good the idea is.

What this idea does not say

Surviving is not hiding.

"Survival first" does not mean not investing. Keeping all your money still has a cost: inflation eats it slowly, which is a real if gradual loss. The point is not to remove risk; it is to choose which risks you take and how big they are.

Nor is it a recipe for the perfect bet. The Kelly formula assumes you know your edge exactly, and in real life you never do. That is why many people who use it bet well below what the formula says. If you err on the side of too much, the penalty is much larger than if you err on the side of too little, as the simulator showed. This connects to the margin of safety: leave room for your mistakes.

To do

Write down your maximum tolerable fall before it arrives.

Try it: write the largest fall you could endure without selling or changing your life, as a percentage. Then calculate how much you would need to gain to recover: the loss divided by what is left. For example, if you lose 30%, 70% is left, and 30 divided by 70 is about 43%. Finally, review the three paths to ruin (everything in one thing, debt, no cushion) and mark whether any applies to you today. If you marked one, that is your first task, before choosing what to buy.
