Can it be undone?

A mistake you can fix costs you money. One with no way back can take you out of the game. Before any big decision, the first question is not how much you can win.

Two kinds of mistake

Not every mistake weighs the same.

If you buy a stock and you are wrong, you sell it, lose something and move on. It hurts, but your money is still there, smaller, and it can grow again. It is like a snowball that shrank but is still rolling downhill.

Other mistakes cannot be fixed. Putting all your savings into a business that closes. Co-signing (promising to pay if the other person doesn't) a debt you later cannot cover. Lending a relative money that never comes back and costs you the relationship on top. Sending money to a scam. These mistakes do not shrink the snowball: they take it off the hill.

This entry is about one question, the one that comes before all the others: if I am wrong, can I go back?

First, stay in the game

No future gain can save you if you have nothing left to play with.

Nassim Nicholas Taleb, a former market trader who writes about risk and chance, insists on a simple idea: the first thing is not to get knocked out of the game. He uses the example of Russian roulette: even if you "win" most of the time, no sensible person plays, because the time you lose there is no next time. A good average is worth nothing if somewhere along the way there is a loss you cannot come back from.

The math is simple. If a bet has a tiny chance of leaving you at zero and you repeat it many times, the chance of surviving all of them keeps shrinking:

chance of staying in the game = (1 − chance of ruin)times you repeat

With a 1% chance of ruin each time, you survive 10 rounds in 90% of cases. You survive 100 rounds in only 37% (computed with the formula). A risk that looks small, repeated over a lifetime, stops being small. That is why irreversible decisions are not judged as a single bet, but as something that adds up with all the ones you will make.

The door metaphor

Some doors open both ways. Others don't.

Jeff Bezos, the founder of Amazon, explained it with doors in his 2015 letter to shareholders. Some decisions are two-way doors: if you walk through and don't like what you see, you come back. Others are one-way doors: if you walk through and don't like what you see, you cannot get back to where you were.

The interesting thing about the letter is that Bezos does not say "be afraid of doors". He says to treat them differently, and that the most common mistake in large organizations is the opposite one: using the slow, heavy process of one-way doors for decisions that can be undone, and so never trying anything.

  • With two-way doors: go ahead, try, be wrong cheaply and correct. Waiting often costs more than being wrong.
  • With one-way doors: go slowly even if the opportunity looks amazing. Ask for another opinion, sleep on it, look for a way to test small first.

Between the two doors

Many decisions can be undone, but at a price.

In real life, almost no door is fully one-way or fully free. Buying a home is the typical example. Technically it can be undone: you sell it. But getting in and out costs money: taxes, paperwork, fees to whoever sells the home, and the time it takes to find a buyer. If you have to move soon, those costs eat much of what you thought you had gained.

The calculator below compares buying a $100,000 home in cash and selling it when you move, against renting for the same years. You set every percentage: the starting values are only there to get you moving and are not data from any country. Look up the ones for your city.

Look at what happens in the first years: the buying line starts high, because the cost of walking through the door is paid all at once, on day one. Renting starts low and grows slowly. The useful question is not "which is better, buying or renting?", which depends on your numbers. It is: how much does it cost me to undo it if I have to leave sooner than I think?

The calculator leaves out important things, and it is worth saying so: there is no mortgage (which makes everything harder to undo), no tax on the gain, and no value for feeling at home, which is real even if it does not fit in a formula.

The trick

Turn one-way doors into two-way doors.

The best answer to an irreversible decision is often neither "yes" nor "no", but "small first". Some ways to do it:

  • Rent before you buy. A year in the neighborhood teaches you more than ten visits.
  • Start small. Before quitting your job to open a business, try it on weekends. Before investing your savings, invest a part you could lose without it changing your life.
  • Leave yourself a way out. An emergency fund turns many one-way decisions into two-way ones, because it does not force you to sell at the worst moment (see lesson 2).
  • Wait a day. If something asks you to decide right now and cannot be undone, that combination is an alarm. Real opportunities almost never expire in an hour.

Common mistakes

Four ways to mix up the doors.

  • Treating everything as irreversible. This is the mistake Bezos points out. If every small decision freezes you, you never try anything, and not trying has a cost too: years without learning.
  • Treating everything as reversible. "If it goes wrong, I'll sell." But some things cannot be sold: a signed debt, money already sent, a broken relationship.
  • Looking only at the gain. A one-way door with a huge gain is still a one-way door. The better the opportunity looks, the slower it pays to go.
  • Forgetting that it adds up. A small risk of losing everything, repeated many times, becomes large. We saw it with the formula above.

How it connects

It is the rule underneath all the others.

In Risk is not a price that moves we saw that what is dangerous is permanent loss. A one-way decision is the most direct road to one. In the entry on surviving first you find the same idea seen from the emergency fund. And in rules, not willpower you will find the tool for these decisions: the pre-mortem, which means thinking about the way back before you walk through the door. The emotional groundwork is in lesson 10, Your worst enemy is you, because irreversible decisions are usually made in a rush, in euphoria or in fear.

Your three big decisions of the year.

Try it: write down the three biggest money decisions ahead of you this year. Next to each one write "two-way" or "one-way". For the one-way ones, write what would happen in the worst case, how much it would cost to undo if you change your mind in a year, and whether there is a way to test small first, like renting in a neighborhood before buying there.
