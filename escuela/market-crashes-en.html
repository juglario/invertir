When the whole market falls.

A big crash is won or lost before it starts. If you already have a cushion, a share in stocks you can stomach and a written plan, you have almost nothing to decide during the storm.

The idea

The outcome of a crash is decided before it starts.

Every so often, the whole market falls. Not one company: almost all of them at once. An index like the S&P 500, which groups 500 of the largest companies in the United States, can lose a third or half of its value in a few months. The highest point before the fall is called the peak, and the lowest, the bottom. When the fall from the peak passes 20%, it is usually called a bear market.

If you invest for thirty or forty years, you will live through several of these. It is not bad luck: it comes with the package. So the useful question is not "how do I avoid the next crash?", which nobody can answer, but "what do I need to have in place so the next crash does not knock me out of the game?".

Nassim Taleb, who wrote several books on risk and chance, sums it up with a line he himself credits to Warren Buffett: in order to succeed, you must first survive. The entry Survival first develops the idea. In a crash, surviving means not being forced to sell cheap. And that is decided by four things you set up on a calm day, never in the middle of a panic:

  • A cash cushion. Emergency money, separate from what you invest, as in lesson 2. If something breaks or you lose an income right when the market is down, you pay with the cushion and not with your stocks.
  • A share in stocks you can really stomach. Not the one you would like to stomach on a euphoric day, but the one you could watch fall by half without selling. The simulator below helps you find it.
  • Money you will not need for years. What you will use soon (next year's college costs, a trip, rent) does not go into stocks. A crash can take years to recover.
  • Rules written down in calm. What you do with your contributions, when you rebalance and what you will not do. On paper, with a date.

Put numbers on it

A percentage does not hurt. Your money does.

"Stocks can fall 50%" is easy to read. Opening the app and seeing your $10,000 turned into $7,000 is something else. The same fact, counted in your money, feels very different. That is why it pays to do the math now, with your numbers, before the crash does it for you.

If you have one part in stocks and another in bonds (loans to a government or a company, which move a lot less, as you saw in lesson 6), the math is simple:

what you lose = stocks × stock fall + bonds × bond move

With $10,000, 60% in stocks and a 50% fall, you lose $3,000 on the stocks. If bonds stay flat, your portfolio becomes $7,000: you lost 30%. To get back to $10,000 you need a rise of about 43% on what is left (3,000 divided by 7,000). With 100% in stocks, you would lose $5,000 and need +100%. With 30% in stocks, you would lose $1,500 and a bit more than 17% would be enough. These are all computed with the formula, not data from the past.

Move the stock share until you find the number you could look at without selling. Be honest: think about how you would feel if that fall lasted two years, with the news saying every day that the worst is still ahead. That stock share, and not the one that gives the highest return on a spreadsheet, is yours. Lesson 9 explains how to build the recipe from there.

The distinction that matters

A loss is only final if you sell.

Howard Marks, co-founder of Oaktree, a firm known for buying the debt of troubled companies when everyone else sells in fear, insists on something that sounds odd at first: real risk is not the price moving. How much a price goes up and down is called volatility. Real risk, for Marks, is permanent loss: losing money and never getting it back.

As long as you do not sell, a market fall is a loss on paper: you own the same pieces of the same companies, it is just that today someone would pay less for them. When you sell at the bottom, you turn it into a real loss, and you miss the recovery that comes after. The entry Risk is not a price that moves explains it in detail.

Two honest caveats. First: this holds for a whole market, bought through an index fund. A single company can go bankrupt and never come back. Second: "if you do not sell, you do not lose" only works if nothing forces you to sell. That is why the four things in the first section are not a detail: they are what makes this sentence true for you.

What already happened

Three big crashes and how long they took to come back.

These are three falls of the S&P 500, measured with closing prices, peak to bottom. The time to come back is counted in price, without dividends (the part of profits that companies pay out to their owners). Someone who collected and reinvested dividends got back to their peak somewhat earlier.

  • 2000 to 2002, the end of the internet bubble: about -49%, from March 2000 to October 2002. It took two and a half years to reach the bottom, and the price only got back to the 2000 peak in May 2007, about seven years later.
  • 2007 to 2009, the financial crisis: about -57%, from October 2007 to March 2009. The price got back to the highest close of 2007 at the end of March 2013, about five and a half years after the peak.
  • 2020, the pandemic: about -34% in just over a month, between February 19 and March 23. It got back to the peak in August 2020, about five months after the bottom.

Notice there is no pattern for the length. One fall lasted two and a half years and another, five weeks. One took seven years to come back and another, five months. Nobody knew in advance. And notice the detail people find hardest: anyone who sold near any of those bottoms missed the recovery.

There is one case worth knowing because it ends differently. Japan's Nikkei 225 index closed at its peak at the end of December 1989 and only beat it again, in price, in February 2024: 34 years. Someone who had all their money in Japanese stocks and needed it during that time did not get it back. A whole market taking that long is possible. That is why "if you do not sell, it recovers" works much better with money spread across many countries, as in lesson 8.

During the storm

Follow the plan: keep contributing and rebalance by rule.

If you set up the things above, you have very little to decide during the fall, and that is good. Your plan says two things:

  • Keep contributing. If you invest part of what you earn every month, in a crash that same money buys more pieces, because they are cheaper. It is the idea in the entry All at once or little by little?, which in a crash works in your favor without you doing anything.
  • Rebalance by bands. If your recipe is 60% stocks and 40% bonds, and stocks fall hard, they end up weighing less: say, 50%. Your rule tells you to sell some bonds and buy stocks until you are back at 60%. It is buying what fell, but by rule, not by courage. The entry Rebalancing with bands explains how to choose the band.

if stocks < target − band: buy stocks until back at target

The simulator below is a made-up crash, with its assumptions written on screen: stocks fall 50% over 18 months, get back to the peak in four and a half years and then grow 7% a year. Four people start with $10,000 in 60/40 and set aside $100 a month. The only thing that changes is what they do.

With the starting assumptions, the math goes like this: all four put in $19,600 in total. The one who sold at the bottom ended with about $16,400, less than they put in, even though the market fully recovered. The one who did not sell but stopped contributing until the recovery ended with about $22,000: nothing broke, but they invested everything they had saved only once it was expensive again. The one who kept contributing reached about $24,400, and the one who also rebalanced, about $24,800.

Look also at what the simulator does not hide: at the bottom, the one who rebalances is a bit lower than the others, because they bought stocks while they kept falling. That is the part that feels bad. Rebalancing does not save you from the fall: it leaves you better placed for the comeback. And try a shorter fall or a slower recovery: the numbers change, but selling at the bottom comes last in every case you can build with these controls.

Looking without guessing

Diagnose where we are, do not predict where we are going.

Marks describes the market as a pendulum that swings from euphoria to panic and almost never stays in the middle. In a hard fall, the pendulum is on the fear side: everyone is selling, nobody wants to hear about stocks, and prices end up below what the companies are worth. His most quoted line on this is: "We may never know where we're going, but we'd better have a good idea where we are."

Notice the difference. Predicting would be saying "the bottom is next week". Diagnosing is saying "today people are extremely scared, so prices are probably cheap compared with what things are worth, even if they can fall further". Nobody knows how to do the first. The second can be learned.

The best example is a column Warren Buffett published in The New York Times in October 2008, in the middle of the crisis, titled "Buy American. I Am." He wrote that fear was everywhere and recalled his rule: be fearful when others are greedy, and greedy when others are fearful. But he also wrote that he had no idea whether stocks would be higher or lower a month or a year later. And he was right not to know: the day the column ran, the S&P 500 closed at about 940 points, and in March 2009 it hit a bottom of about 677, roughly 28% lower. Buffett did not guess the bottom. He read the market's mood and acted with money he would not need.

What not to do

Five mistakes that turn a fall into a loss.

  • Waiting for the bottom to buy. The bottom is only visible on the chart afterwards. Whoever waits "for it to drop a little more" is often still waiting when the market is already up 30% from the bottom. If you want to buy more in a crash, let your rule (the bands) say so, not your gut.
  • Borrowing to invest. With debt, a fall you could wait out becomes one you cannot: the lender asks for the money right when everything is down, and you have to sell at the worst moment. Debt is the shortest road from volatility to permanent loss.
  • Checking your portfolio every hour. The more often you look, the more losses you see and the more you feel like doing something. During a crash, looking on your rule's dates is enough. Deleting the app from your phone for a few weeks is a valid strategy.
  • Changing strategy midway. Moving everything to cash "until things calm down" is selling at the bottom under another name. If your plan turned out to be too risky for you, change it, but after the storm, calmly and in writing. The entry Rules, not willpower explains how to tie yourself to the mast.
  • Having everything in one country. Japan shows that a whole market can take decades. Spreading across countries does not prevent crashes, which tend to be global, but it lowers the chance that yours is the one that does not come back.

Your plan, in three stages

Before, during and after.

Before, on a calm day:

  • Build the emergency cushion and keep it outside your investments.
  • Choose your stock share with the simulator above: the one you could watch fall by half without selling.
  • Write down your recipe, your bands and your monthly contribution. Put two dates a year in your calendar to review them.

During the fall:

  • Keep contributing the same amount, on the same dates.
  • Rebalance only if a part left its band, and on the date your rule says.
  • Look rarely. If you feel like selling, reread your plan and wait a week before doing anything.

After, when it passes:

  • Write down what you felt and what you did. It is the most valuable data you will have about yourself for next time.
  • If you found your stock share was too much, lower it now, calmly. If you had calm to spare, do not raise it out of euphoria: wait a year.
  • Check the cushion: if you used it, refill it before investing more.

What happens in your head in these moments is covered in lesson 10. What your plan does is make sure that head does not have to decide alone.

To do

Write your plan for the next crash.

Try it: on a sheet of paper, write today's date and fill in five lines. One: how many months of expenses I have in my cushion. Two: what share of my money is in stocks and how much I would lose, in dollars, if they fell by half. Three: how much I contribute each month and on what day. Four: my rebalancing band. Five: the sentence I will read when I feel like selling, for example "this loss is on paper as long as I do not sell". Keep it where you will find it the day the market falls.
