Cheaper is not always safer.

If the reason you bought has stopped coming true, a cheaper share price does not protect you. It may be telling you the business changed.

A mistake told by the person who made it

The price kept falling and every drop looked like a bargain.

A margin of safety means buying something well below what you think it is worth, so you have a cushion if you are wrong (we cover it in this other entry). The idea is good. The trouble starts when you use it backwards: the price falls, you feel the cushion getting bigger, and you buy more. But if what fell is not only the price but the business, there is no cushion. You are buying, cheaper, something that is worth less.

Steve Eisman, an investor known for betting against mortgage loans before the 2008 crisis, told a story about one of his own mistakes in a 2025 talk. He owned shares of Citigroup, a large US bank. His central idea was that the bank's profitability (how much it earns for each dollar of its own capital) would climb back to the level of its competitors. That idea failed, and it failed in plain sight. He stayed in anyway, for a long time, because every drop looked like a bargain. Years later he saw it differently: the market was not wrong, it was putting the right price on a business that had broken.

Eisman gave the mistake a name: thesis creep. There is no day when you decide to change your mind. The idea shifts little by little, without you noticing, until the only thing left standing is the price.

How it happens

The price replaces the facts.

A thesis is a short list of concrete things that have to be true for an investment to work. It is not "I like the company". It is something like "sales grow more than a certain number" or "fewer than so many customers leave each year". Each point can be checked when the company publishes its numbers, which listed companies do every three months in their quarterly report.

The thesis creeps when those points stop showing up and, instead of changing your mind, you change your argument. Before, you said "I buy because profitability will rise". Now you say "I buy because it fell 40%, it cannot be worth this little". Notice that the second sentence says nothing about the business. It talks about the price. And the price alone does not tell you whether something is cheap or whether it was right for it to fall.

Eisman's fix is mechanical, not psychological. It is not about promising yourself to stay cool. It is about writing down in advance:

thesis = 2 to 4 facts about the business, each with a number and a deadline
the thesis breaks when the facts fail, whatever the price does

Written like that, the price moves to second place. It can fall a lot without the thesis breaking (and then it may really be a bargain), and it can barely move while the thesis is already broken.

Try it

Banco Modelo: two rules, the same path.

Banco Modelo is a made-up bank. We follow 12 quarters (three years) of its life. You write the thesis with two facts you set with the controls and one fixed fact:

  • Fact 1: return on equity is at least a certain percentage per year.
  • Fact 2: bad loans, the loans people stopped paying on time, stay below a certain percentage.
  • Fact 3: deposits, the money people keep at the bank, keep growing.

Two people buy $1,000 in quarter zero. The first follows a price rule: every time the share is 40% below their last purchase, they put in $1,000 more (up to three times) and never sell. The second follows a thesis rule: sell everything when at least two of the three facts fail, and keep the money in cash. Under the chart you can see, for each quarter, how many facts failed.

What the simulator shows

The thesis rule does not always win. It wins when it matters.

On the path where the thesis breaks, the price rule ends up putting in four times more money and losing more than half of the total (the simulator computes the exact numbers). Every extra purchase felt like grabbing a deal. It was really new money going into a business that was breaking. The thesis rule loses too, because it sells after the price has already fallen, but it loses little and with a single purchase.

Now switch to the temporary setback. The bank has two ugly quarters and then recovers. Here buying the dip worked, and if you wrote the facts too tightly (for example, the thesis breaks after a single bad quarter) you sold right before the recovery. This is honest and there is no point hiding it: from inside quarter 2 or 3, the two paths look almost the same. Eisman admits it: a great company going through a real but temporary rough patch can look, from the outside, exactly like a thesis that is breaking.

So what is the rule for? So that facts, not the price, decide the difference. In the setback, bad loans rise for one quarter and come back down, and deposits never stop growing. On the broken path, all three facts fail together and keep failing. Asking for the facts to fail two quarters in a row is a simple way not to react to a single noisy number. Try both options and watch how the exit changes.

The hard part

Write facts that can fail.

The rule only works if the thesis is written so that it can turn out wrong. A sentence like "good company, good management, it will come back" never fails, whatever happens. That is exactly why it is thesis creep's favorite hiding place. A useful fact has four parts:

  • It talks about the business, not the price. Sales, customers, debt, bad loans, margins. Not "the share has to reach this much".
  • It has a number. "Grow a lot" cannot be checked. "Grow more than 10% a year" can.
  • It has a deadline and asks for persistence. One quarter can be noise. Two in a row already say something.
  • It says which one leads. If you have three facts and one passes, one fails and one was not published, decide in advance which matters most. If you decide afterwards, you will pick the one that suits you, and that is thesis creep too, in miniature.

Common mistakes

Five ways a thesis creeps without warning.

  • The only rule is a price. "I don't sell below $X" can work as a brake against panic selling. But if it is the only rule, the price is already the argument. Thesis rule first, price rule second, as an alarm.
  • Reinterpreting the rule every time. If every time something happens you have to explain to yourself again what your rule meant, you have not written it yet. A written rule is read, not renegotiated.
  • Treating an opinion as a fact. An analyst changing their mind can be a good reason to review your thesis. It is not the review. The review is the business's numbers.
  • Moving the number afterwards. You wrote "bad loans below 4%", it came out at 4.4%, and now you say 5% is fine too. If you change the line, do it with a written reason and before the next number, not after.
  • Believing the rule will always make you sell at the right moment. It won't. You will sell late on broken theses and sometimes sell too early on setbacks. What it saves you from is the worst outcome: putting more and more money into something that is no longer what you bought.

How it connects

It is the same idea as real risk, seen up close.

In Risk is not a price that moves we saw that the real danger is permanent loss, the kind that does not come back. Thesis creep is the most common way to turn a drop into a permanent loss: not by selling at the bottom, but by buying more of something that broke. That is why the two entries are read together.

It also connects with familiarity bias, because a thesis creeps more easily in a company you know and like, and with rules, not willpower, because writing the facts in advance is exactly that: a structure that decides for you when you are nervous. If you are missing the emotional groundwork, start with lesson 10, Your worst enemy is you.

To do

Three facts, one sheet of paper, the next report.

You don't need money invested to practice. It is enough to pick a company you find interesting and follow it for a year.

Try it: pick a company you own or like. On a sheet of paper, write three facts about the business, not about the price, that would make you change your mind. Each with a number, a deadline and "two quarters in a row". Mark which of the three leads. Keep it, and when its next quarterly report comes out, grade each fact as "met", "failed" or "not published", without changing any number.
