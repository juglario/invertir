A return without a currency says nothing.

A big number is not always a gain. To know whether you really grew, you need to know which currency you measure in and how much prices rose in that currency.

The idea

Having twice the pesos is not having twice as much.

Imagine you put your savings in a peso time deposit that pays 100% in a year. At the end you have twice the pesos. It sounds like a huge gain. But if prices rose 120% that same year, what used to cost $100 now costs $220. With your $200 you buy less than before.

There are two different numbers here. What you were paid is the nominal return: the one on the bank's sign. What you gained in things you can buy is the real return. The first is measured in bills. The second is measured in purchasing power, which is the only thing that matters when you go to the supermarket.

The rule of this entry is simple and gets forgotten all the time: a return without the currency it is measured in, and without that currency's inflation, says nothing.

Fisher's math

The real return is divided, not subtracted.

The American economist Irving Fisher wrote down, more than a century ago, the relationship between the nominal rate, inflation and the real rate. The exact version is a division:

1 + real = (1 + nominal) / (1 + inflation)

With the example above: (1 + 1.00) / (1 + 1.20) = 2 / 2.2 = 0.91. Subtract 1 and the real return is −9%. You lost about 9% of purchasing power, even though you have twice the bills.

Many people use a shortcut: rate minus inflation. With a 5% rate and 3% inflation the shortcut says 2%, and the exact math gives 1.94%. Almost the same. But with a 100% rate and 120% inflation the shortcut says −20%, when the truth is −9%. With high inflation the shortcut is wrong by a lot. In high-inflation countries it pays to always do the full calculation.

The second trap

When your currency loses against another, you lose too.

Devaluation is when your currency loses value against another: each dollar starts costing more pesos. It works like inflation, but measured against another currency instead of against supermarket prices.

Example: your peso investments rose 50% in a year, but the dollar went from 1,000 to 1,600 pesos. If you had 1,000,000 pesos, that was 1,000 dollars. Now you have 1,500,000 pesos, which are 1,500,000 / 1,600 = 937.5 dollars. In dollars you lost 6.25%, even though in pesos you "made" 50%.

Argentine history has the clearest example. Between 1991 and early 2002, by law, one peso was worth one dollar (this was called convertibility). It was abandoned in January 2002, and by the end of that year it took about 3.4 pesos to buy a dollar, according to International Monetary Fund data; in mid-2002 it got close to 4. The same bills, from one year to the next, came to be worth less than a third of a dollar.

The dollar is not a safe either

Measuring in dollars helps, but the dollar has inflation too.

That is why many people in high-inflation countries measure in a strong currency, like the dollar or the euro, which loses value much more slowly. It is a good habit, with one caveat: the dollar also loses purchasing power. The Federal Reserve, the central bank of the United States, targets inflation of 2% a year, and in some years it was quite a bit more.

Dollars kept in a drawer for ten years, with 3% inflation a year, end up buying about 26% less (the math is 1 / 1.03 to the power of 10, which gives 0.74). Losing slowly is still losing. To know whether you really grew in dollars, take off US inflation too, with the same Fisher division.

You start with the equivalent of 100 dollars. One option: a local-currency time deposit that pays a rate. The other: dollars in a drawer, which earn nothing. Move the rate, local inflation, devaluation and dollar inflation. The chart measures everything in today's dollars; the numbers next to it also show how each saving looks measured in local prices.

Notice a rule that comes out of the math: for the local deposit not to lose against the dollar, the rate has to be at least the devaluation. And for it not to lose against local prices, it has to be at least local inflation. They are two different races, and you can win or lose them separately.

The underlying rule

Measure in the currency you will spend in.

No currency is "the right one" for everybody. The useful question is: which currency will the things you are going to pay for be in? If you are going to live in euros, your goal is in euros. If you spend in pesos day to day but big things, like a car or a house, are thought of in dollars, the dollar is your ruler for those goals.

The danger is not owning things in another currency. The danger is a mismatch: saving in one currency and spending in another without having thought about it. Someone who saves in pesos to pay for something in dollars in two years is betting, whether they know it or not, that the rate beats devaluation. Someone who saves in dollars to pay next month's rent in pesos is betting on the opposite.

For your emergency cushion (see lesson 2) what matters most is the currency of your monthly spending. For distant goals, what matters is the currency those goals will be paid in.

Common mistakes and limits

Four ways to fool yourself with a number.

  • Comparing rates from different countries. 40% in one currency and 4% in another cannot be compared directly. First turn everything into the same currency, or into purchasing power.
  • Looking at the monthly rate and multiplying by 12. With compound interest, 5% a month gives about 80% a year, not 60% (1.05 to the power of 12 is 1.80). See the magic of compound interest.
  • Forgetting that the past does not repeat evenly. The simulator uses the same rate, inflation and devaluation every year. In real life they change, sometimes all at once, as in 2002.
  • Believing that measuring in dollars is the same as being protected. Measuring is one thing; what you own is another. A company that sells things people need and can raise its prices defends your purchasing power better than idle money, in any currency.

Your turn

Your year, measured three ways.

Try it: find out how much you had saved a year ago and how much you have today. Work out three things: (1) how much you grew in your local currency, (2) how much you grew in dollars, converting both numbers at each date's exchange rate, and (3) how much you grew in purchasing power, dividing by the year's inflation with Fisher's math. Did you grow in all three, in some, or in none?
