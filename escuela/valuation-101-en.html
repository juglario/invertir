Valuing is telling a story with numbers.

A good valuation joins a story about the company with the numbers that make it possible. And even the most famous number, the P/E, can fool you if you do not know how to read it.

The idea

Neither a fairy tale nor an empty spreadsheet.

Aswath Damodaran, a finance professor at New York University and one of the people who has done most to teach how to value companies, puts it this way: a story without numbers is a fairy tale, and numbers without a story are an empty spreadsheet.

Valuing starts with the story. What is this company and what will it become? Why will people keep buying from it? Then comes the uncomfortable part: how large would sales, margins and the money left free have to be for that story to be true? And is that believable? If the story says "it will be huge" but the numbers it needs are impossible, the problem is not the spreadsheet: it is the story.

Valuing is not guessing tomorrow's price. It is estimating how much a business is worth for the money it will generate over its whole life, so you can then compare that with what is being asked for it (see Price, value and margin of safety).

The mechanism

A business is worth the cash it will hand you, brought to today.

The underlying idea has a long name: discounted cash flow. It has two parts.

The first is free cash: the money the company has left each year after paying salaries, suppliers, taxes and whatever it needs to invest to keep running. It is what, in theory, it could hand out to its owners.

The second is the discount. A dollar ten years from now is worth less than a dollar today, for two reasons: you could invest today's dollar and have it grown, and the one ten years away is not certain. So each future dollar is divided by (1 + r) as many times as there are years to go, where r is the discount rate: what you ask of that investment for waiting and for the risk.

value = cash year 1 / (1 + r) + cash year 2 / (1 + r)² + ... + what comes after

That "what comes after" is called the terminal value: a way of summing up all the distant years by assuming cash grows slowly forever. Further down you will see that it is usually the largest part of the value, and the hardest to know.

Try it

Build your story and see what it is worth.

An imaginary company sells 100 a year today. Choose how fast its sales grow for 10 years, what share of each sale becomes free cash and what rate you discount at. After year 10, cash grows 2.5% a year forever. The red line is the price the market asks. It is a toy model: it is good for seeing the shape, not for valuing a real company.

Three things to watch. First, the bar on the right: with the starting values, more than half of the value comes from after year 10, that is, from years nobody can see. Second, the rate: going from 9% to 10% takes about 15% off the value, without the company changing at all. Third, growth: at 5% a year the value is about 286, at 15% it is about 608. The story you tell weighs more than any decimal.

The most famous shortcut

The P/E is a valuation squeezed into one number.

The P/E (price over earnings) tells you how many years of today's earnings you pay when you buy a share. A P/E of 20 means you pay 20 times what the company earns in a year. It looks simple, but inside it hides an entire valuation.

If a company did not need to reinvest and its cash grew at a rate g forever, the math above simplifies a lot:

price / next year's cash = 1 / (r − g)

With r = 8% and g = 3%, the multiple is 1 / 0.05 = 20. With g = 5% it is 33. With g = 6% it is 50. Notice what happens: as the assumed growth gets close to the discount rate, the multiple shoots up. One more point of growth "forever" can justify a much higher price. That is why high multiples are so sensitive: any small disappointment in expected growth makes them fall hard.

An important warning: growing costs money. To sell more, a company almost always has to invest. If that investment earns less than it costs to raise the money, growing subtracts value, even as sales rise. Damodaran insists on this: growth only adds when what the company earns on what it invests beats its cost of capital.

The question in reverse

Do not ask what it is worth. Ask what has to happen.

There is a more honest way to use the model: instead of inventing a growth rate and seeing what value it gives, you start from the price and ask what growth is needed to justify it. This is called a reverse DCF. The good part is that you do not have to guess the future: you only have to judge whether what the price takes for granted looks believable to you.

Try raising the price and watch the growth it needs turn heroic. Sales four or five times larger in ten years do happen, but few companies pull it off. If the price needs that, you have no room to be wrong.

Two P/E traps

Same number, different story.

First trap: the trailing P/E can be inflated by earnings that do not repeat. The P/E of the last twelve months uses earnings that already happened. If in that period the company booked an extraordinary gain (it sold a building, or an investment it held in another company went up in value), the P/E looks low even though the business is not earning more. An example with made-up numbers: a share costs $100 and the company earned $5 per share last year, so its trailing P/E is 20. But $1.50 of those earnings came from selling a piece of land, which will not repeat. Without it, the business earned $3.50 and the real P/E is 100 / 3.5 = 28.6. The clue: if the forward P/E (based on expected earnings) is higher than the trailing one, the market expects earnings to fall.

Second trap: cheap against its own history is not cheap against rates. The inverse of the P/E is called the earnings yield: what each dollar invested gives you, measured in earnings. If the market trades at 18 times earnings, each dollar gives you 1 / 18 = 5.6%. If the 10-year US Treasury bond pays 5%, the gap is only 0.6 points. If it paid 2%, with the same P/E the gap would be 3.6 points. Same P/E, a very different reward for the risk of owning stocks instead of bonds (see the lesson on bonds). These are example numbers: look up today's before drawing conclusions.

These two cases are dated examples from the public market, not recommendations: they are there to train your eye.

Common mistakes and limits

What the model cannot do for you.

  • False precision. A spreadsheet with forty tabs and two decimals knows no more than you do about the future. What matters is the shape: what growth, for how long, at what rate.
  • Forgetting the share count. Value is split per share. If the company keeps issuing new shares, your slice shrinks even as the business grows.
  • Using the P/E on cyclical companies. In a company whose earnings rise and fall with a cycle (for example, those that sell raw materials), the P/E looks lowest right at the best point of the cycle, when earnings are at their peak. Cheap at the peak is not cheap.
  • Confusing price and value. Damodaran separates "pricing" (what the market pays today, with its mood) from "valuing" (what it is worth for its cash). When the price falls, the question is whether the value fell, and those are two different questions.
  • Falling in love with the huge market. A giant market is part of the story, not a cash number. See The huge market that fools you.

Your turn

One P/E, one rate and one question.

Try it: pick a company you know and look up its trailing P/E and its forward P/E on a quotes site. Work out 1 divided by the P/E and compare it with the 10-year US Treasury rate. Then write the company's story in one line and use the second simulator: what yearly growth is the price taking for granted? Do you believe it?
