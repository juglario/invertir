All at once or little by little?

With a large sum, investing it all at once usually wins. But entering gradually, on fixed dates, protects you from yourself. Both things are true at once.

First, the right question

Investing part of your salary each month is not a decision. A large sum is.

Let's separate two situations that often get mixed up. If each month you have part of your salary left over and invest it, you are not choosing a strategy: you are investing money when you have it. That is fine and there is nothing to decide.

The real question appears when you already have a large sum available at once: a bonus, an inheritance, the sale of something, or years of savings. There are two paths:

  • All at once (called lump sum): you invest the whole amount today.
  • Little by little (called dollar-cost averaging): you split the sum into equal parts and buy one part per month, on fixed dates, for a set period. What you have not invested yet waits in a safe account.

Both end with the money invested. The difference is what happens in the meantime.

What the data says

All at once won about two times out of three.

In 2012, Vanguard, one of the largest fund managers in the world, published a study whose title states the conclusion: "Dollar-cost averaging just means taking risk later". It used historical data from the United States, the United Kingdom and Australia. It compared investing everything at once in a mix of 60% stocks and 40% bonds against spreading the same sum over 12 monthly instalments. In all three countries, lump sum won about two times out of three.

The reason is simple. Markets rise for more years than they fall. Money waiting in cash to enter gradually is, during that time, without most of its return. Averaging in is, in effect, carrying less risk for a few months, and so on average it earns less too.

But "two out of three" means that one time out of three, spreading won. And those times are not random: they are mostly the cases where the market fell right after you went in.

Try it

Four thousand invented years, one at a time.

This simulator does not use historical data: it invents 4,000 market cases with the return and volatility (how much the price shakes) you choose. In each one it compares investing everything on the first day against spreading it in equal instalments over the months you pick, with the waiting cash earning a low rate. Then it counts how often each approach won, and by how much.

Play with the controls and notice three things:

  • With a higher expected return, lump sum wins more often. If the market tends to rise, waiting costs you. If you lower the return to zero, the advantage disappears.
  • With more months of spreading, the typical gap grows. The longer the money waits, the more return is lost on average.
  • With more volatility, the extreme cases get bigger on both sides. Look at the shape of the chart: usually the difference is small, but there are cases where one approach wins by a lot.

An honest note: with the starting assumptions the simulation gives about two in three, similar to Vanguard, but that depends on what you choose. It is a model, not history.

What the average does not show

The times lump sum loses are the ones that scare people most.

So does averaging in not help? It helps with something else. Look at three possible paths for the year after you enter with $1,000:

On the "falls right after" path, whoever went in all at once watched about a quarter of their money disappear in four months. Whoever was spreading had only a part invested, and also bought the later instalments more cheaply. That is the scene that frightens, and the one averaging spares you. On the other paths, lump sum tends to win because more money worked for longer.

The key point of this entry: the worst result is not that of someone who picked the "wrong" of the two methods. It is that of someone who went in all at once, saw the fall, got scared and sold. That turns a temporary drop into a permanent loss, as Risk is not the price moving explains. If investing everything today would leave you awake at night and selling at the first fall, a gradual plan you actually follow beats a perfect plan you abandon.

If you choose gradual

Make it a plan, not "I'll wait until it drops".

Averaging in works only if it is automatic. If each month you decide again whether to buy, you are no longer averaging: you are trying to guess the market, which is exactly what the pendulum teaches you not to do. Three rules:

  • Fixed dates. Pick the day of the month and write it down. Better still, schedule it.
  • Fixed amounts. Split the sum into equal parts. Do not buy "more if it falls" or "less if it rises".
  • A short period. For example, 3 to 6 months. As the simulator showed, the longer the spreading, the more expected return you set aside.

Meanwhile, the waiting money should sit somewhere safe that earns something, not in an account that inflation erodes.

What this entry does not say

It is not a rule for money you will need soon.

All of this is for money you will leave invested for many years. If you will need it in one or two years, the question is not all at once or gradually, but whether it should be in stocks at all. And before any large sum comes the emergency cushion: if you do not have one, that money's first destination is that cushion.

Nor does it say that lump sum will win this time. Two in three is a frequency, not a promise: nobody knows whether you will get one of the two outcomes or the other. So the decision is not only about numbers; it is also about what you will be able to stick to.

To do

Decide today, calmly.

Try it: imagine you receive $6,000 today to invest for the long term. Write on paper what you would do: all at once, or six purchases of $1,000 on exact dates. Then write what you would do if, a month after entering, everything fell 20%. If your honest answer to the second question is "I sell", choose gradual and schedule the purchases today. If it is "I do nothing", all at once has the numbers on its side.
