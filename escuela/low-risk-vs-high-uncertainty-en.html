Low risk is not the same as low uncertainty.

Not being able to guess next year's earnings does not mean you can lose your money for good. Mixing the two up makes you overpay for the boring and underpay for the lumpy.

Two words that get confused

Uncertainty is not risk.

Uncertainty is not knowing how much a company will earn next quarter or next year. Risk, in this sense, is the chance of losing your capital for good. They are different things. A company can have results that are impossible to predict and, at the same time, be very hard to push into bankruptcy.

The investor Mohnish Pabrai says the market mixes the two. It overpays for companies with smooth earnings, even if their advantage is fragile or they carry debt. And it underpays for companies with earnings that jump around, even when they have lots of cash, no debt and a lasting advantage. What the price reflects is the noise of the next number, not the odds of ruin.

Why the discount exists

Many managers are judged every quarter.

Many fund managers are paid or lose their jobs based on how they did in the last few quarters. If a company has a bad year because of a cycle or a large purchase, the pressure pushes them to sell. That creates a discount: the price falls because of short-term noise, and the discount lasts as long as no one with a longer horizon appears.

That is Pabrai's logic: to collect that difference you have to be able to sit through the jumps. It is a recurring inefficiency, not a one-time stroke of luck.

Try it

Two companies with the same expected value, and different risks.

Imagine two companies that, on average, are worth the same five years from now, starting from $100. Company A has a lumpy business, but a lot of cash and no debt: even if things go badly, the cash sets a floor. Company B has a steady business, but with debt: in a bad year it can lose much more. The chart shows how the possible futures spread out for each. It is a simulation with assumptions, not data from real companies.

Move A's cash and B's debt. Press "Another batch of futures" and notice that the average barely moves, but the left tail, where the losses are, does. That is the point: what differs between the two is not the expected value, it is the risk of ending up very low.

The floor

Cash is the floor; debt is a ceiling that can break.

Cash in a company without debt gives it a cushion. If the business has a bad year, the company is not forced to sell anything at the worst moment, nor to pay interest that suffocates it. That is why the balance sheet matters as much as the business.

Debt does the opposite: the more debt, the more chance a bad year turns into a payment problem. With debt, a fall in the business is amplified in the value of the equity, and the loss becomes hard to recover.

Marks' test

Could you estimate earnings per share five years out within ±50%?

Howard Marks proposes a way to measure uncertainty instead of just feeling it. The test: if you estimate that earnings per share (total earnings divided by the number of shares) will be $10 in five years, can you say with reasonable confidence that it will land between $5 and $15? If you can, the uncertainty has limits: it is high but bounded. If you cannot, what you have is not a business you understand, but a story.

This ±50% band is not a magic number. It is a way to force yourself into an honest range. Below you can see how the band changes with how lumpy the business is, how many years you look ahead, and how much debt the company carries.

Notice what happens when you raise the debt. With more debt, the same business becomes more unstable per share, and the band loses coverage. That is why the balance sheet is always checked, not only when you buy.

What it looks like in practice

Money that comes back before the uncertainty is resolved.

The underlying idea is that the ideal case returns your money before the future clears up. Pabrai tells examples of this kind, such as a company with a lot of cash, no debt and unpredictable earnings, that at a low price recovered much of what was paid within a few years. We have not verified those specific cases here; use them as illustrations of the pattern, not as proof.

The pattern to look for is simple: if the business gives you back your investment within a few years even in its bad scenario, the market is charging you for an uncertainty you do not really bear.

Common mistakes and limits

When the cushion gets spent.

  • Believing the cash is forever. The idea breaks if the company spends its cushion on a bad purchase, on buybacks paid with debt or on huge investments. That is why the balance sheet is reviewed regularly, not only when you get in.
  • Confusing "lumpy" with "fragile". Earnings that jump are not the same as a company that can go bankrupt. Keep the two questions separate.
  • Using the test dishonestly. If your estimate always lands exactly on the number that suits you, you are not measuring uncertainty: you are inventing it.
  • Forgetting that debt amplifies in both directions. With debt, what goes up goes up more, and what goes down goes down more. That is why the balance sheet check comes first.

Your turn

Estimate, write it down and come back in five years.

Try it: pick a company. Look up its earnings per share for the last five years, and its current cash and debt. Write down your estimate of its earnings per share in five years, and ask yourself whether you would bet that the result lands between half and one and a half times your number. If you cannot, the real question is no longer the price: it is whether you understand the business well enough to have an opinion.
