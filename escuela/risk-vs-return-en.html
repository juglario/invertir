Risk is not a price that moves.

Most people measure risk by how much a price goes up and down. That has a name (volatility), but it is not what can leave you without money.

Two words people mix up

Volatility and permanent loss are not the same thing.

Volatility is how much a price shakes around. A stock can fall 30% in a month and recover in a year. Someone who didn't sell lost nothing real: they just watched the number go down.

Permanent loss is something else: money that leaves and does not come back. Howard Marks, an investor who has been writing letters to his clients for decades, says that real risk is that loss. And he says it is most likely right when everything looks safe, because people get overconfident and pay any price.

In 2020, the S&P 500 (about 500 of the biggest companies in the United States) fell by about a third in a few weeks, and anyone who didn't sell was back at the high within a few months. That was volatility. Someone who owned shares of Lehman Brothers, a bank that went bust in 2008, lost almost everything and never got it back. That was risk.

Why drops weigh more than gains

Losing 50% takes a 100% gain to fix.

If you have $100 and lose half, you are left with $50. To get back to $100 you need to gain $50 on $50: that is +100%. Big losses take longer and longer to recover, because the base you have to rebuild is smaller.

The exact math: if a drop is d and growth afterward is g per year, the years to get back to the starting point are

years = ln(1 / (1 − d)) / ln(1 + g)

With growth of 7% per year, a 20% drop takes about 3 years to recover. A 50% drop takes about 10. An 80% drop takes almost 24.

Try it

Move the drop and watch two people.

Both have $100 invested in the same thing. Both see the drop. One holds on and the other sells at the bottom, and ends up with cash that earns nothing. After the drop, the market grows at whatever pace you choose.

What the simulator shows

The drop is not the problem. Selling during it is.

As long as you don't sell, the drop is a number on a screen and the time to recover is the cost. When you sell at the bottom, the drop becomes permanent. That is why Marks talks about the risk of loss and not about price: what decides your result is whether you have to sell, not how far the price fell.

This connects to lesson 10: your biggest enemy is often urgency. That is why the practical rule is to have a cushion before you invest (see lesson 2). A cushion lets you avoid selling, even when the price is at the floor.

The price you pay is part of the risk

An excellent company bought at a very high price can be risky.

Here is Marks's most counterintuitive idea: risk depends not only on what you buy, but on how much you pay for it. When everyone believes a business is perfect, the price rises until it reflects that perfection. If something then turns out just a bit worse than expected, the price falls a lot, even though the business is still good.

In the 1970s there was a group of very admired companies, called the "Nifty Fifty" in the United States. The idea was to buy them and never sell, because they were the best. Several fell between half and more than 80% during 1973 and 1974, and some took years to get back to their high. Nothing changed suddenly in the business: what changed was what people were willing to pay.

The same works in reverse: a weak business bought cheap can be less risky than an excellent one bought at a high price. Marks sums it up by saying the risk is in overpaying, not in accidentally choosing badly. So, before asking yourself whether a company is good, ask what you are paying for it and what would have to happen for the price to make sense.

How to spot a permanent loss early

Four signs worth checking.

There is no magic formula, but there are questions that separate a passing drop from a deeper problem. Look at these four, on paper, before you buy and again every time the price moves sharply:

  • How much debt does it have? A company with a lot of debt can go bust even if its sales are doing fine, because it can't pay on time. Lehman had enormous debt compared to its capital.
  • Does it depend on a single product or a single customer? If everything rests on one thing, a change in technology or the loss of one customer can leave you with nothing.
  • Is it diluting? If the company keeps issuing new shares, your piece of the business gets smaller even if the company grows.
  • Can you sell without losing everything you have? If the answer is "no", the real risk is not the price, it is your situation. Having a cushion changes that.

None of these questions has a perfect answer. What makes them useful is that they force you to look at the business and not just the chart.

Something to think about

A drop that didn't force you to sell didn't make you lose.

That doesn't mean everything recovers. Lehman did not recover because the business broke. So the question is not just "how far can it fall?", but "what would have to happen for it to never come back?".

Try it: pick a company or a fund you have in mind. Write down two things: (1) how much it could fall in a bad year, and (2) what would have to break for it to never come back. If you can't write the second one, the risk you are worried about may not be the one you have.
