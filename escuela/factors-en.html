Factors: what a stock is made of.

A factor is a trait many stocks share that explains why they move together. Some have paid a premium for decades, but none pays it every year.

The idea

A stock is a bundle of traits.

Look at any stock and you will see a pile of features: how cheap it is compared with what it earns, how much it rose in recent months, how much money it makes for every dollar invested in it, whether it is big or small, how much its price swings (that is volatility). Every stock is a different bundle of those traits.

Finance researchers found something useful: a handful of those traits explains a good part of why stocks move together. If two companies are very different (one sells sneakers, the other makes software) but both are cheap and small, they often go up and down at the same time. Those shared traits are called factors.

The short version: some kinds of stocks behave alike. If all your stocks are the same kind, you do not really have many stocks. You have one bet repeated several times.

The most studied

Five traits research has looked at closely.

  • Value. Companies that are cheap compared with what they earn or own. Eugene Fama and Kenneth French documented it in two papers from 1992 and 1993. Over the long run cheap stocks did better than expensive ones, but in the United States they lagged for most of 2007 to 2020, more than a decade of patience.
  • Momentum. What rose most in recent months tends to keep rising for a while, and what fell most tends to keep falling. Narasimhan Jegadeesh and Sheridan Titman published it in 1993. The problem is the "crashes": when the market rebounds hard after a crisis, the losers jump and the factor flips all at once.
  • Quality. Profitable, stable companies with little debt. Robert Novy-Marx showed in 2013 that companies earning a lot on what they have invested did better. Careful: quality bought at any price stops being a good idea.
  • Size. Small companies earned more in history. Rolf Banz published it in 1981. After publication the premium shrank a lot, and today people debate whether it is still alive outside the tiniest, hardest-to-buy companies.
  • Low volatility. Calm stocks earned about as much as jumpy ones, with fewer scares. That is the opposite of what textbook theory predicts, which says more risk should pay more.

Notice that "growth" is not on the list. The fastest-growing companies tend to be the expensive ones, the other side of value, and in the main academic models that trait did not pay a premium of its own. Growing is good for a company; paying any price for growth is not always good for you (see Valuing is telling a story with numbers).

The premium and the noise

A small premium with a lot of noise takes years to show.

Saying a factor "pays a premium" means that, on average, it beat the market by a few points a year. But that average comes mixed with noise: some years it wins by a lot, others it loses by a lot. That year-to-year difference against the market is called tracking volatility.

When the premium is small and the noise is large, the premium gets buried. If a factor beats the market by 2 points a year on average, but in any single year it strays 12 points up or down, then in one year its chance of losing to the market is almost the same as its chance of winning. It takes many years for the premium to peek out above the noise.

With a normal approximation (the bell curve), the average over N years is spread around the premium p, with a deviation that shrinks with the square root of the years:

chance of trailing over N years ≈ Φ(−p × √N / v)

Here Φ is the cumulative probability of the bell curve, p is the premium per year and v is the tracking volatility per year. With p = 2% and v = 12%, the math gives about a 43% chance of trailing in one year, about 30% over ten years, and it takes about 59 years for that chance to drop to 10%. These are numbers computed from those assumptions, not real history.

What it feels like from the inside

The premium is paid in exchange for sitting through long droughts.

A probability is abstract. What a person lives through is a single history, year after year. The second simulator builds one possible 30-year history: each year the factor beats or trails the market according to the premium and noise you choose. The line shows what the factor is worth compared with the market (at 100, they are level). The red part is the longest stretch the factor spent below its previous best.

Press "Another possible history" several times. You will see histories where the factor wins comfortably and others where it spends ten or fifteen years without beating its best moment, with exactly the same assumptions. Both are the same strategy. The difference is the luck of the order of the years.

This happened for real. Value in the United States had its long drought between 2007 and 2020. Momentum had one of its worst moments in 2009: according to Kent Daniel and Tobias Moskowitz, between March and May of that year the stocks that had fallen most rose 163% while those that had risen most gained only 8%, and a strategy that bought winners and sold losers sank.

The label and the contents

A factor fund holds whatever its rule picked last time.

Some funds buy stocks according to a factor. A momentum fund, for example, picks the stocks that rose most every so often and keeps them until the next review. S&P 500 momentum funds usually review twice a year.

That has a consequence few people look at: if the stocks that rose most were almost all tech, the fund looks like a tech fund, even if the name does not say so. And since it reviews every six months, it can sit for months with yesterday's winners after they have started to fall. Even a "value" fund can end up with tech giants among its largest holdings if its method mixes several criteria.

The name is the label. What you own is what is inside. If you already have a lot of one sector and you buy a factor fund that, because of how the market has gone, is full of that same sector, you did not diversify: you repeated the bet (see Do not put all your eggs in one basket).

Common mistakes and limits

What the chart of the past does not tell you.

  • Published premiums shrink. David McLean and Jeffrey Pontiff studied 97 traits that predicted returns and found that, after publication, their returns were on average 58% lower. When everyone knows a trick and it is cheap to use, part of the premium disappears. Expect less than history shows.
  • In a crisis, everything moves together. Factors that looked independent can fall at the same time when there is panic.
  • There are hidden factors. Expensive growth stocks, low-volatility stocks (many are utilities or real estate companies) and bonds suffer together when interest rates rise. A portfolio that looks spread out can be a single bet that rates will fall.
  • Costs eat the premium. Momentum buys and sells a lot, and every trade costs something. What it earns on paper does not always reach your pocket.
  • Selling in the worst drought is the surest way not to collect it. A factor you cannot hold through its worst moment is a factor you will sell right there, which is what happens with any investment (see Risk is not the price moving).

How to use it

First to diagnose, then to choose.

The healthiest use of factors is not chasing whichever did best last year. It is looking at what you already own with new eyes. Sort your investments by trait, not by name: how many are expensive and have been rising hard? How many depend on rates falling? If two different investments share the same dominant trait, for your pocket they are almost one.

And if you ever pick a factor fund, do it knowing three things: the expected premium is small, the noise is large, and the bad stretch will come. If you know that ahead of time, it is much easier not to sell at the worst moment. This connects with Rebalancing with bands, a rule for not letting one bet grow on its own until it dominates your portfolio.

Your turn

Look inside before trusting the name.

Try it: go to the page of a factor fund (value, momentum or quality) and write down its 10 largest holdings and its weight by sector. Do the same with an S&P 500 fund. How similar are they? Then go back to the first simulator, set a 2% premium and a 12% deviation, and write down how many years the math says you need. Would you be willing to wait that long without selling?
