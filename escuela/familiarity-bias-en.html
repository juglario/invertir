Knowing a company is not having an edge.

Knowing a lot about a company does not tell you whether its price already reflects everything you know. And if you also work there, one bad year can hit you twice.

The comfortable trap

What you know feels safer. It isn't always.

We tend to invest in what is close to us: the company we work for, the sneaker brand we wear, the bank in our own country. That closeness feels reassuring, and reassurance feels like conviction. Economists call it familiarity bias: giving something more weight just because you know it, even though knowing it does not tell you whether it is worth buying at today's price.

The key is to separate two kinds of knowledge. One is knowing how a company works: whether its product is good, whether people love it, whether its employees are happy. The other is knowing how much of all that is already in the price. Millions of people know a famous brand is good. That is exactly why that fact is almost never an edge: if everyone knows it, the price already reflects it.

What the data show

Companies everyone knows also fall from the top.

Charlie Bilello, an analyst who publishes a yearly collection of market charts, uses Nike as an example. It is a brand half the world knows. According to his data, over five years the stock fell more than 70% from its peak while the S&P 500 (an index of about 500 of the largest US companies) went up. Nobody stopped knowing Nike. What changed was the business and what the market was willing to pay for it.

Bilello adds another fact that works as a general rule: of the ten largest US companies in 1990, none is still in the top ten today, and of the 2000 list only Microsoft remains. Being huge and famous does not guarantee staying on top ten or twenty years later.

The hardest case is Enron, a US energy company that went bankrupt at the end of 2001 after an accounting fraud. According to a report by the US Congressional Research Service, at the end of 2000, 62% of the money in its employees' retirement plan was in Enron's own stock. The share had passed 90 dollars in August 2000 and was worth less than 1 dollar when the company filed for bankruptcy. Thousands of employees lost their jobs and much of their retirement savings in the same year, from the same cause.

Try it

When your pay and your savings depend on the same thing.

Your paycheck is, in a way, an investment: it pays you something every month, like a bond paying interest. The important question is who pays it. If the same company pays your salary and is also a large part of your savings, both of your sources of money fall together in the same scenario.

Move the controls with your own numbers or made-up ones. The simulator takes a bad year for your company: the stock falls as much as you choose and, if you want, the company cuts jobs and you spend some months without pay. Meanwhile you keep paying your expenses from savings. It compares this with the same person, with the same months out of work, but holding none of their company's stock.

Notice one thing: the biggest hit is almost always the paycheck, not the stock. That is why holding a lot of your own company is riskier for you than for a stranger who buys the same share. The stranger loses the stock part and keeps getting paid somewhere else. You lose both.

The counterpoint

Concentrating is not always wrong.

It would be easy to stop here with "never hold a lot of one company". But some very good investors do exactly that on purpose. Mohnish Pabrai, an investor who follows Warren Buffett's school, puts a lot of money into a few ideas when he believes he knows something the market has not priced in yet. This style has a name in this school: concentration.

What separates informed concentration from familiarity bias is not how much you hold, but why and with what rules:

  • Informed concentration: a written thesis, concrete facts that would disprove it, information the market lacks that you keep up to date, and a size decided in advance.
  • Familiarity bias: a lot of money in something because you like it, because you use the product or because you work there, with none of the above.

Watch the "keep up to date" part. Someone who worked at a company five years ago may know things that are no longer true. Inside knowledge expires.

To check yourself

Three questions that tell one from the other.

Think of a company where you have, or would like to have, a lot of money. Answer honestly. No answer forces you to sell: the goal is to see clearly what kind of bet you are making.

Common mistakes

Four sentences that sound like an edge and are not.

  • "I use it every day." So do millions of others. Using a product tells you it is good, not that the stock is cheap.
  • "I work there, I know what it is like inside." It can be a real edge. But it is also the situation with the most double-hit risk, and the one that clouds you the most, because the company is part of your identity.
  • "I don't sell below such a price." A price rule is not a thesis. If it is your only rule, you are already in the trap of thesis creep.
  • "It is a company from my country, I understand it better." It is the same bias at country scale. Many people keep almost all their savings in their own country, which is also where they earn their pay and where they live. If that country has a bad year, the hit arrives twice again.

None of these sentences says the investment is wrong. They say the reason you gave is not enough.

What to do if you are already concentrated

You don't need to sell everything. You need to see it and give it rules.

Many companies pay part of the salary in their own shares. If that happens to you, holding them is not a mistake: it is part of your job. What you can do is set rules in cold blood:

  • A written cap. Decide what share of your savings can be in your company. When it goes over, sell the excess, without arguing about it again every time.
  • A cushion outside. Your emergency fund has to be outside your company, because you will need it exactly on the day the company is doing badly. It is the idea of lesson 2.
  • A yearly review. Once a year, rewrite your thesis and the facts that would disprove it. If you can't, the real reason may be comfort.

This connects with rules, not willpower: it is not about promising yourself to be objective about your own company, which is nearly impossible, but about setting rules that work even if you are not. And the foundation of it all is lesson 8, Don't put all your eggs in one basket.

To do

A list with one uncomfortable column.

Try it: make a list of the companies you own or would like to own. Next to each one, write why. If the reason is "I use it" or "I know it", add a column: "what do I know that the market doesn't". If the column stays empty, you have already learned something. If you work at one of those companies, put your numbers into the double-hit calculator and write down how many months of expenses you would have left.
