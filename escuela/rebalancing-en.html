Rebalancing with bands.

Over time, whatever rises most eats your recipe. Rebalancing puts it back, and works best with a rule that acts only when something has really drifted.

The problem

Your recipe drifts on its own, even if you touch nothing.

In lesson 9 you built a recipe: for example, 60% stocks and 40% bonds. Stocks are small pieces of companies, which rise and fall quite a lot. Bonds are loans you make to a government or company, which move less. You chose that 60/40 because it is the risk you can live with.

The problem is that the two parts do not grow at the same pace. Suppose you have $6,000 in stocks and $4,000 in bonds. A good year, stocks rise 25% and bonds 3%. Now you have $7,500 and $4,120: stocks weigh about 65%, not 60%. Nobody decided that. It just happened. This shift is called drift.

And drift has a direction. Stocks, on average, grow more than bonds over many years, so if you do nothing your recipe fills up with stocks. After years you may hold 75% or 80% in stocks without having chosen it, just when everything is rising and people feel confident. That means more risk than you wanted, at the worst moment to hold it.

The fix

Rebalancing means returning to the recipe.

Rebalancing means selling a little of what grew past its weight and buying what fell behind, until you are back on the recipe. In the example, of $11,620 in total, 60% is $6,972. You sell $528 of stocks and move it to bonds. You are back at 60/40.

stocks you should hold = total × target percentage

Notice what this rule does without you noticing: it makes you sell a little of what rose and buy what lagged. It is the opposite of what emotion asks for. In euphoria it makes you sell a little; in panic, buy. It is an automatic way of not following the market's pendulum.

An important clarification: rebalancing is not a way to earn more. Sometimes it ends with more money and sometimes with less, because it leaves less time invested in whatever rose most. Its job is different: to keep the risk you take the same as the risk you chose.

The usual question

How often? A band is better than a date.

There are two ways to decide when to rebalance. One is by calendar: once a year, for example, whatever happens. The other is by bands: you choose a margin around your target and act only if a part moves outside it. With a 5-point band and a 60% target, you do nothing while stocks stay between 55% and 65%.

Why not rebalance all the time? Because every sale costs something. There are fees, the gap between the price you can buy and sell at, and sometimes taxes on what you gained. It is worth paying that to fix a real drift, not to chase noise. Vanguard's 2010 study on rebalancing found no single ideal frequency for everyone: calendar-based and band-based approaches worked similarly. Bands of around 5 points are a common choice, not a figure from the study.

The simulator below uses the same invented market for four rules: never rebalance, once a year, a 5-point band and a 10-point band. That way the only thing that changes is the rule.

Look at the table under the chart. Rebalancing once a year makes you trade 30 times in 30 years, even when the drift was tiny. The 5-point band keeps the recipe almost as close with fewer trades. The 10-point band trades very little but lets the recipe drift much further. None is the "right" one: it is a trade-off between precision and cost. Try another invented market: without rebalancing, the final result is sometimes higher and sometimes not, but the stock weight usually ends far from what you chose. Across 1,000 repetitions with the starting assumptions, unrebalanced stocks ended more than 10 points away from 60% in 74% of cases.

A detail for small parts

The 5/25 rule.

The 5-point band has a problem with small parts of the recipe. If something weighs 10%, drifting 5 points means ending at 5% or 15%: half or one and a half times what you wanted. That is too much. So many people use the 5/25 rule, popularised by the author Larry Swedroe: rebalance when a part drifts 5 points or 25% of its own weight, whichever comes first.

  • For a 10% part, 25% of 10 is 2.5 points. The band runs from 7.5% to 12.5%.
  • For a 40% part, 25% of 40 is 10 points, but 5 comes first. The band runs from 35% to 45%.
  • For a 70% part, it is still 65% to 75%.

The cheapest option

Rebalance without selling anything: with new money.

If you are still contributing, there is a way to rebalance that costs almost nothing: send the new contribution to the part that is short. You sell nothing, so you pay no selling fees or taxes on sales.

An example: you have $10,000, with $7,500 in stocks and $2,500 in bonds (75/25), and your recipe is 70/30. A $1,000 contribution arrives. The new total is $11,000, and 70% is $7,700. You send $200 to stocks and $800 to bonds. You end with $7,700 and $3,300: exactly 70/30. Try it with your numbers:

If the drift is very large, one contribution is not enough, and the calculator shows how much is still missing. Then you have two options: wait for future contributions or sell part of the position. If the drift is inside your band, waiting is fine.

When not to follow the rule

The rule buys what fell. Before that, ask why it fell.

Rebalancing makes most sense with funds that hold a whole market, like an index fund. If a whole market falls, it is very unlikely to disappear. With a single stock it is different. If a company you hold has fallen a lot, the rule asks you to buy more to restore the percentage. Before doing it, ask: has the company changed, or only the price? If the business broke, buying more to hit a number is throwing good money after bad. The entry Risk is not the price moving explains why.

And one rule that never bends: the emergency cushion is never used for rebalancing. It sits outside the recipe precisely so that you never have to touch it because of an investment decision.

To do

Write your rule before you need it.

Try it: write your recipe on paper, with a band for each part (5 points or 25% of its weight, whichever is smaller). Pick two fixed dates a year to review it. Next to it, write where your next contribution goes if any part leaves its band. When the date comes, do not think: look at the rule and do what it says.
