The market's pendulum.

Markets swing between euphoria and panic and rarely rest in the middle. You cannot guess when they will turn, but you can recognise roughly where we are.

The picture

The market moves like a pendulum and rarely stops in the middle.

Howard Marks is an American investor who has written letters to his clients for decades, and many read like lessons. One of his best-known ideas compares the market to a pendulum. At one end is euphoria: "all is well, nothing can go wrong". At the other is panic: "all is bad, nothing can go right". The middle, the reasonable zone, is where the pendulum spends very little time. It almost always swings toward one of the extremes.

What is interesting is that companies, in general, do not change as fast. A supermarket chain sells roughly the same in a euphoric year as in a panic year. What changes a lot is what people are willing to pay for it. That is the point of Price, value and the margin of safety: the price moves with the mood, and value moves much more slowly.

Why it overshoots

Prices change the mood, and the mood changes prices.

The engine of the pendulum is not only the economy but psychology. It works like a wheel that feeds itself:

  • Prices rise, and the people who bought feel clever.
  • Those who did not buy feel they are missing out, and they jump in.
  • Because everything is rising, people take less care and banks lend more easily.
  • That new money pushes prices even higher, and the wheel turns again.

On the way down the same happens in reverse: prices fall, people get scared, sell, nobody lends, and prices fall further. Nothing inside the wheel stops it at the right point. That is why the pendulum overshoots in both directions.

A real example: the Nasdaq index, which includes many technology companies in the United States, peaked in March 2000, at the height of the internet boom. It then fell sharply, and only closed above that peak again in April 2015, about fifteen years later. Many of those companies survived and grew. What had gone to an extreme was the price people paid for them.

Try it

Buying in euphoria or buying in fear.

The simulator invents twenty years of market. Value grows steadily, 5% a year. The price is that value multiplied by the crowd's mood, which swings back and forth like a pendulum with some randomness. It then compares three people: one who buys only when there is euphoria, one who buys only when there is fear, and one who buys every month without looking.

The result repeats across almost any simulation: buying when everyone is happy makes you pay high, and buying when everyone is afraid makes you pay low. So far it sounds easy. The problem is that the chart shows twenty years at once. In real life you stand in one month, not knowing what comes next. That is why the person who buys every month, without trying to guess, ends up near value: they do not beat the pendulum, but they do not let it beat them either.

Diagnose, do not predict

Three questions to see which way the pendulum leans.

In 2001, Marks wrote a memo whose title sums up his stance: "You can't predict. You can prepare." We cannot know when the pendulum will turn. But we can look around and see which side it leans toward. He suggests three questions:

  • How much optimism is there? If everyone talks about what they made, even people who never invested, the pendulum is on the euphoria side.
  • How afraid of losing are people? When nobody thinks about losing, riskier things get bought without asking for anything in return. When everyone is afraid, even safe things are sold cheap.
  • How easy is it to borrow? When credit is offered without questions, money is looking for places to go, and that pushes prices up. When nobody lends, the opposite happens.

Lots of optimism, little fear and easy credit are signs for caution. The reverse usually hides opportunity. Try the three questions with the pendulum below.

The reading does not tell you "sell everything" or "buy everything". Marks talks about adjusting your posture a little: taking somewhat less risk when the pendulum is very high, and somewhat more when it is very low, always within your plan. It is a thermostat, not a switch.

Why it is so hard

The right move feels wrong exactly when you have to make it.

If all this is so clear, why does not everyone do it? Because at the critical moment it feels the other way round. In euphoria, selling or buying less feels like being the fool who missed the party. In panic, buying feels like throwing money into a hole. Your head feels what everyone feels, and that is exactly what has to be overcome. The lesson 10, Your worst enemy is you, is about this.

Two honest warnings:

  • Arriving early looks a lot like being wrong. The pendulum can keep rising for years after it seems excessive. Someone who said "this is expensive" during a boom may have been right, but prices kept rising for a while before the fall.
  • The bottom does not announce itself. In panic, the cheap can get cheaper. That is why nobody buys "at the bottom" on purpose: you buy in pieces and with a plan.

How to use it without going crazy

Let a rule do what your head cannot.

The simplest way not to follow the pendulum is to avoid having to decide in the moment. Three rules that do that work for you:

  • Contribute the same amount every month. As the simulator showed, buying evenly keeps you near value without guessing.
  • Rebalance by a rule. If your recipe says 60% stocks and euphoria pushed it to 70%, the rule makes you sell a little of what rose most. The entry Rebalancing with bands explains it. It is an automatic way of going against the pendulum.
  • Keep a cushion. In panic, the person who does not need to sell can wait, and even buy. Someone who needs the money sells at the worst price. Survival first explains this.

If you are interested in the school of thought behind this, see the cycles school.

To do

Keep a pendulum journal.

Try it: this week, collect three signals: the day's economic headlines, what people are saying about investing in your groups or feeds, and how many easy-loan offers reach you. Use them to answer Marks' three questions from 1 to 10, enter the answers in the tool above and note the result with the date. Repeat in three months and compare. It is not for predicting: it is so that the next time you feel euphoria or fear, you have a page written by a calmer version of yourself.
