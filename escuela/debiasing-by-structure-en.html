Rules, not willpower.

Your biases do not go away because you know about them. They are held back by rules written on a calm day and by someone who checks you before you press the button.

Knowing is not enough

Biases come factory-installed.

A bias is a mental shortcut that makes us err always in the same direction. You have already seen several in this school: the fear that makes you sell at the bottom, the pride that will not let you admit your thesis broke, the comfort of the familiar. The question of this entry is what to do about them.

The intuitive answer is "learn about biases so you don't fall for them". It sounds good and works poorly. Emily Haisley, who heads behavioral finance at BlackRock, one of the largest investment managers in the world, puts it this way: biases are not a lack of information. They come with the brain. Under stress, without sleep, or watching your money shrink, the part of your head that thinks long term works worse, whatever you know.

So instead of promising yourself to be more rational, it pays to set up your surroundings so the bias has less room. Discipline is not summoned in the hard moment. It is written beforehand, on a calm day, and then followed.

An old idea

Ulysses did not trust his willpower. He had himself tied to the mast.

In the Odyssey, the Greek poem, Ulysses has to sail past the sirens, whose song makes sailors steer their ship onto the rocks. He does not try to resist. He asks his sailors to plug their ears with wax, to tie him to the mast and not to untie him even if he begs. The calm Ulysses beats the tempted Ulysses because he decided first.

Economics found the same thing with data. In 2001, the economists Brigitte Madrian and Dennis Shea studied a US company that changed a single thing in its retirement savings plan: instead of having to sign up, you were signed up unless you asked to leave. The people were the same and so was the information. But participation among new employees rose by about 50 percentage points. Nobody's willpower changed. The structure did.

structure = a rule written in advance + something that enforces it when you don't feel like it

Try it

Three people, the same fall, three reflexes.

All three start with $100 split the same way: 70% in stocks and 30% in something safe that earns little. The market follows a simulated, random path with a sharp fall at some point. The only difference is what each one does:

  • Do nothing. Watches the fall and touches nothing.
  • Panic-sell and come back. When the market falls a certain percentage from its peak, sells all their stocks. Buys back when the market has already risen a certain percentage from the bottom, "once things calmed down".
  • Rebalance by rule. Once a year, without looking at the news, goes back to 70% and 30%. If stocks fell, that forces them to buy stocks. If they rose a lot, to sell some.

Move the two controls and press "Another path" several times. The starting path is one of many. Under the chart, the simulator repeats the same rules on 500 different paths and counts what happened.

What the simulator shows

Panic is not a strategy. It is a coin toss.

The first thing you will see is uncomfortable and worth saying: in this model, panic-selling and coming back does not always lose. Sometimes it beats doing nothing, because it sold before the fall went on. But look at the 500-path count: it ends worse roughly half the time, and on many paths the gap is large in one direction or the other. Panic does not give you an edge. It swaps a result close to the market's for a bet, and to win it you have to be right twice: when to get out and when to come back.

Raise the "buys back" control and see what happens. The longer you wait to come back, the more often you end up worse. And in real life people who sell in fear do not usually come back by a fixed rule: they come back when they feel safe, which is almost always after the price has already risen. There is a study that measures something similar with real data: Morningstar, a company that analyzes funds, calculates each year how much investors earn compared with what the funds they invest in earn. In its 2025 edition, for the ten years to 2024, the average investor earned 7.0% a year against 8.2% for the funds. Morningstar explains it mainly by getting in and out at bad moments. Careful: other researchers dispute that the whole gap is due to bad timing decisions, so take it as a clue, not a law.

The third person, the one who rebalances, is not smarter than the other two. She has a written rule that forces her to do the scary thing (buy after a fall) without having to decide it at the worst moment. In this model she ended above doing nothing on most paths, but that depends on the assumptions: in markets that rise a lot and steadily for years, not rebalancing wins. Her real advantage is something else: she never had to fight her own fear. If you want to see rebalancing in detail, it has its own entry.

The toolbox

Four structures that work even when you are nervous.

  • A checklist. Before any important purchase or sale, you always answer the same questions: why am I doing this?, what would have to happen for me to realize I was wrong?, can it be undone?, am I calm, or deciding in anger, fear or a rush? A list does not think for you. It forces you to think.
  • A devil's advocate. Someone you trust who gains nothing from your decision and whose job is to find the hole in it. One detail matters a lot: they give their view before hearing yours. If they speak after, they unintentionally adjust to what you think.
  • The pre-mortem. It is an idea from the psychologist Gary Klein, who explained it in the Harvard Business Review in 2007. Instead of asking "what could go wrong?", you imagine a year has passed, the decision turned out terribly, and you write down why. Klein cites a 1989 study which found that imagining something has already happened helps people come up with more reasons (about 30% more) for why it might have happened. More reasons does not mean correct reasons, but it does mean fewer blind spots.
  • A default answer. If you have to decide something by a date, also write down what happens if you don't decide: "if I haven't chosen by Friday, I stay as I am". That way, not deciding becomes a decision you made, not something that happened to you.

Notice that none of them asks you to be braver or colder. They all move the decision to a moment when you think better: beforehand, in writing, and with another person watching.

Try it

Build your pre-mortem before deciding.

Think of a money decision ahead of you, even a small one. Write it down, imagine it went wrong and answer the questions. The tool gives you back a simple reading and a summary to copy by hand.

Common mistakes

How a good structure breaks.

  • Writing the rule in the middle of the storm. A rule invented while the market is falling is not a rule: it is your fear formatted as a list. It is written on a calm day.
  • Looking for a devil's advocate who says yes. If you catch yourself looking for someone to agree with you, you probably already know the answer.
  • Skipping the rule "just this once". Exceptions always feel special. If you want to change a rule, change it in cold blood and in writing, and make it apply to the next decision, not this one.
  • Making forecasts and never checking them. If you write "I think this will happen", also write when you will check it. A forecast without a review date is an opinion, and it teaches you nothing.

How it connects

The other entries tell you what to do. This one, how to stick to it.

Writing the facts of your thesis (thesis creep), capping your company in your savings (familiarity bias) and slowing down at a one-way door (can it be undone?) are all rules. This entry explains why you need rules and not good intentions. The groundwork is in lesson 10, Your worst enemy is you, and the emergency fund from lesson 2 is the simplest structure of all: it lets you not sell when fear tells you to.
