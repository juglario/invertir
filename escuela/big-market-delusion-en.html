The huge market that fools you.

A market being huge does not mean the companies fighting for it will make money. Often it is the sign that everyone is investing as if they will win.

The trap

Everyone assumes they will win.

Aswath Damodaran, a finance professor in New York, describes a trap that keeps repeating. A huge market appears. In English it is called the TAM (total addressable market, the total size of the possible business). Every company that enters assumes it will capture a large share. All of them invest as if they were going to win. They cannot all win.

What happens next is fairly predictable: more capital comes in than the business can ever return, price wars start, margins shrink, and the most indebted companies are the first to break. The market can be real. The mistake is that each player believes it will be the winner.

So "the largest market I have ever seen" is an alarm, not a reason to buy. A huge size attracts everyone, and the more who enter, the less is left for each one.

The math

Size × share × margin × multiple.

To avoid being swayed by the impression, it helps to break a company's value into four parts. First, the size of the market: how many sales per year there are in total. Second, the share the company keeps. Third, the profit margin: how many cents of each dollar sold stay as profit. Fourth, the multiple: how many years of that profit the market is willing to pay.

value = market × share × margin × multiple

Because everything is multiplied, every assumption counts in full. If the share halves, the value halves. If the margin shrinks in a price war, the value shrinks by the same proportion, even if the market keeps growing. And the multiple, the easiest number to inflate, can multiply everything else.

Try it

The chart shows the company's value by the share of the market it keeps, with the other assumptions fixed. The red line is the value that today's price takes for granted. See what share it would take to justify it. The numbers are a simulation with assumptions, not data from any real company.

The lesson of the chart is the shape of the curve: value is proportional to the share you win. An error of 5 points in the share can be an error of 25% in the value. And since everyone assumes a large share, the sum of the assumed shares tends to be bigger than the market.

What does not add up

If each one assumes it wins, the sum goes over 100%.

This is the heart of the trap. Imagine several companies fighting for the same market. Each, in its plan, assumes it keeps a reasonable share, say 25%. With six companies, the assumed shares add up to 150%. That is impossible: the whole market is 100%. Someone is wrong, and it is not just one.

That means the value the market places on the group today cannot fully exist. Part of that value has to disappear, either because each one's share falls or because margins fall for everyone. No company has to be bad for this to happen: it is enough that all of them are reasonable and ask for the same thing.

Raise the number of companies and watch the sum pass 100%. Press "Other assumptions" and you will see that, even with variations, the result holds: when many believe they are the winners, the sums do not close.

How the party is financed

A bubble funded with equity is not the same as one funded with debt.

How a boom is financed matters as much as how long it lasts. The late-1990s internet bubble was funded mostly with shares. When it fell, the Nasdaq, the US technology index, dropped about 78% between March 2000 and October 2002. It was a huge blow, but it stayed in the stock market.

An investment funded with debt, by contrast, can break loans. If the debt is not repaid, the damage reaches the banks, the people who lent, and the real economy. So before believing a boom, ask: where is the money to build this coming from?

An example from the current debate: the largest technology companies are spending very large amounts on data centers for artificial intelligence, and part of that spending is financed with debt. Analysts disagree on whether it will pay off. There is no settled answer, and this entry does not give one.

Where the risk hides

Three places to look before trusting a big number.

Steve Eisman, the investor portrayed in Michael Lewis's book and the film about the 2008 crisis, points to places where risk often hides. These are general ideas that apply to any company:

  • Few customers. Sales can look spread out but depend on a handful of buyers. If one leaves, a large part of the business leaves with it.
  • Debt off the balance sheet. Some companies set up separate entities or contracts so debt does not show up directly. Look for those obligations in the notes to the annual report.
  • Gains that are not from the business. If a company owns shares in another company and that one rises in value, the gain can show up in results even though nothing was sold. That gain does not repeat.

Howard Marks, another investor who writes letters to his clients, gives a similar warning: when a company sells to another and then lends it money or invests in it so it can buy more, the loop can look like real growth when it is only money going around in circles.

Common mistakes and limits

Not every big market is a trap.

  • Thinking size is the thesis. A big market is the starting point. What matters is whether the company can turn a slice of it into cash, with sustainable margins.
  • Dismissing everything new. Some big markets really grew and left winners. The problem is not the size, but how many competitors you have to assume will win.
  • Forgetting the advantage. Pabrai asks whether there is a lasting advantage under the market: a brand, a low cost, a user network. A crowded market with a real advantage is different from a crowded market without one (see Low risk is not the same as low uncertainty).
  • Trusting the price as proof. A price that rose a lot does not prove the business is sound. Often it only proves everyone is looking at the same thing.

Your turn

Three questions before you believe the story.

Try it: take a company that talks a lot about its "huge market". In its latest report look for: (1) what share of sales comes from its largest customers, (2) how much debt it has and what long-term commitments, and (3) whether part of the profit came from investments rather than the business. Then use the sum simulator: if its competitors assume what it assumes, how much do they add up to?
