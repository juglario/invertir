Now, the trick with a roulette wheel ↓

The math they don't show you

The house doesn't have luck. It has math.

A roulette wheel has 37 numbers: 18 red, 18 black and one green, the 0. If you bet on red and red comes up, they pay you double, as if it were fifty-fifty. But it isn't fifty-fifty.

When the 0 comes up, everybody loses. That little zero is the casino's paycheck. It means that, on average, the casino keeps 2.7% of everything that gets bet. You won't notice it in one spin. In a thousand spins, it's the only thing that matters. In online slots and sports betting apps, the house's cut is usually even bigger.

It's not bad luck for the ones who lose or talent for the ones who win. The little zero plays in every single spin, and time makes it show up. The problem is that people don't bet just 10 times. They bet every day.

Sports betting

Knowing a lot about soccer isn't enough.

Pretend game: the Hawks vs. the Pumas. The Hawks are the favorites. Who wins? Pick one.

Now look at what those numbers are hiding. Each one (the "odds") tells you how likely the house thinks each result is: it's 1 divided by the odds. 1 ÷ 2.00 is 50%, 1 ÷ 4.00 is 25% and 1 ÷ 3.00 is 33%. Add them up.

100%

50% + 25% + 33% = 108%. In real life, the three results together add up to 100%: one of them has to happen. That extra 8% is the house's cut, tucked inside the odds.

That's why, even if you know a lot and guess right often, you lose in the long run: they pay you as if each result were more likely than it really is. A study of bettors who knew a lot about hockey found they picked winners better than chance, but they didn't win more money. The odds already include what you know.

If it happens to a friend, or to you

Asking for help isn't losing. It's the smartest move.

Signs that something isn't right

  • They think (or you think) about betting almost all the time.
  • They bet to win back what they lost.
  • They lie about money or borrow money to play.
  • They get grumpy if they can't play.

If it's a friend: don't scold them or make fun of them. Tell them you care, and help them tell a grown-up they trust. If it's you: tell someone in your family, a teacher or your school counselor. If you don't know how to start, copy this:

I want to tell you something that's hard for me to say: I've been betting (or a friend has been betting) and I don't know how to stop. Can you help me?
