The memo, to get started

To start saving, first know how much you need and what for.

A short guide for someone who earns a salary and wants to get organized: the goal, the cushion, the instruments, sample portfolios and how not to ruin them. It takes about 15 minutes to read.

1. The goal

Monthly spending, times 12, times 20.

The basics: lesson 1, Why does money lose value?.

To start saving, you need to know how much and what for. If you are saving to retire, a classic goal to start with is what you would need to live for 20 years. The math is simple: what it costs you to live for one month, times 12 months, times 20 years.

If you spend 1,000 dollars a month, that is 12,000 a year and 240,000 as the target for your investment fund. Do the math in a strong currency, like dollars or euros. In a currency with high inflation, like the Argentine peso, inflation moves your goal every month and you never know if you are getting closer.

It looks like a mountain, but you do not climb it alone or all at once. If you save every month for decades, compound interest ends up putting in roughly half. You can see it in the calculator.

The goal to start with 20 years

1,000 a month gives you 240,000. If the money is spent without earning anything, it lasts exactly 20 years.

If you want it to last forever 25 years

The "4% rule" says that, once invested, a portfolio can handle withdrawals of 4% a year, adjusted for inflation, for about 30 years in almost all of US history. That takes 25 years of spending: 300,000 in the example.

Source for the 4% rule: William Bengen, "Determining Withdrawal Rates Using Historical Data", Journal of Financial Planning, 1994. It is a study based on US data. The past does not guarantee the future.

2. How saving really works

Pay yourself first. What is left over never is.

The basics: lesson 3, How to save without suffering.

How do you reach the goal? Little by little. Set aside 15 to 20% of your take-home pay at the start of each month, on payday, and do not touch it except in exceptional situations. If you wait until the end of the month to see what is left, the answer is almost always "nothing".

It is the 20 in the 50/30/20 rule: half for what you need, 30% for what you enjoy and 20% for your future self. It is not a law, it is a starting point.

1

Do not rely on willpower.

Set up an automatic transfer to another account on payday. What you do not see in your everyday account, you do not spend. The idea is old: in The Richest Man in Babylon (1926) the advice was to keep "one coin in every ten" before spending anything.

2

Measure for a month before you judge yourself.

Write down everything you spend for one month, without changing anything. Only then do you know your real number, and that is the one that goes into the goal. Almost everyone is surprised by the small expenses that keep repeating.

3

Expensive debt first.

If you only pay the minimum on your credit card, the interest rate they charge you is higher than any return you will get by investing. Paying off that debt is the best investment available. Then, the cushion. Then, investing.

4

If 15% is not possible, start with 5%.

The habit matters more than the percentage. Start with what you can and raise it one point every time you get a raise. That way you never feel like you are losing something.

4. The emergency fund

The first thing you fill is the cushion.

The basics: lesson 2, First, the cushion.

What you set aside each month goes first to an emergency fund, in dollars and always within reach. Once the cushion is full, everything you set aside goes to the investment fund.

1

How much: 3 to 6 months, and more over time.

The common rule is to save 3 to 6 months of expenses. With a salary in a Latin American country, where I write from, I would start with 6 (6,000 dollars in the example) and over time grow it to 1 or 2 years (24,000). In the US, since 1945 recessions have lasted about 10 or 11 months on average, but finding a new job can take longer than the recession, and in countries like ours crises tend to come one after another. You do not need to wait until you have 2 years to invest: once you reach 6 months, you can keep adding a smaller amount to the cushion and send the rest to the investment fund.

2

Where: safe, but not locked away.

This money goes into low-volatility things that are available when you need them. For example, if you have a year of cushion: 6 months in cash or in an account that pays interest and lets you withdraw the same day, and the rest in short-term deposits, money market funds or short-term Treasury bills. The idea is for it to earn something without being hit by market swings.

3

Not all in one place.

If you reach a year of cushion, split it up: 6 months in one place, 3 months in one bank and 3 in a different one. One of those places can be in another currency, like the euro. In December 2001, in Argentina, the "corralito" left deposits trapped in the banks. A split cushion does not prevent a crisis, but it keeps a single closed door from leaving you with nothing.

The cushion is not there to make money. It is there so you never have to sell your investments at the worst moment.

5. The instruments

Lending or owning. That is the difference that matters.

The basics: lesson 5, What is a stock? and lesson 6, What is a bond?.

Almost everything you will see is one of two things: you lend money to someone, or you buy a small piece of something.

When you lend Term deposits, bonds, corporate bonds

They promise you a rate and give your money back on a set date. You earn little, but it is more predictable. The risk is that whoever owes you does not pay, or that inflation eats up the rate.

When you own Stocks, ETFs, CEDEARs

Nobody promises you anything. If the companies earn more, you earn more. In bad years you can see drops of 30% or more. Over the long run, it is what has paid the most.

6. The investment fund

Investing well is hard. So invest passively.

The basics: lesson 7, Buying the whole market at once.

First, let's be clear about something: investing is hard. A sophisticated strategy takes a lot of reading and time, and the people who do it well usually do it as a full-time job. The goal here is different: to invest passively, without spending much time on it.

That is what index funds, or ETFs, are for. Instead of picking companies, you buy all the companies in a market. For example, the VTI fund includes every US stock: when you buy VTI you own a small piece of thousands of companies at once. You can buy ETFs directly through a brokerage account with a regulated broker, such as Interactive Brokers, which works internationally. If you are in Argentina, local brokers like Balanz or Cocos also work. Compare fees before opening the account: with small amounts, a fixed fee weighs a lot.

1

Why it works.

Most funds that pick stocks by hand earn less than the index they are trying to beat. In the US, about 9 out of 10 large-cap stock funds fell behind the S&P 500 over 15-year periods, according to S&P's SPIVA report. The index wins because it charges little and never makes picking mistakes.

2

Buy every month, no matter what.

Buying a fixed amount every month is called DCA (dollar-cost averaging). When the market is expensive you buy fewer units, when it is cheap you buy more, and you get rid of the impossible question of when to get in.

3

Time is what protects you.

A fund can lose money in the short term. But the US stock market, with dividends reinvested, has never had a 20-year period with a loss since 1926. Be careful about thinking that holds everywhere: Japan's stock market took 34 years, from 1989 to 2024, to get back to its peak. That is why it pays to buy broad markets, not bet on a single country.

4

What they charge matters.

Large ETFs charge between 0.03% and 0.20% a year. A fund that charges 2% sounds like little, but over 30 years it eats up about half of what you would have earned. Always check the annual cost (expense ratio).

Sources: S&P Dow Jones Indices, SPIVA U.S. Scorecard. Historical US stock return data since 1926 (Ibbotson/Morningstar). Nikkei 225: the December 1989 peak was surpassed in February 2024.

7. How to build the portfolio

Four sample portfolios.

The basics: lesson 8, Do not put all your eggs in one basket and lesson 9, Build your portfolio.

None of them is "the best". Pick the one you can stick with without selling when it falls.

A widely recommended strategy is the three-fund portfolio: one fund with stocks from your own market, one with stocks from the rest of the world and one with bonds. The idea is that they complement each other, so if one falls for a while, the others soften the blow. If you are in Argentina and buying through CEDEARs, the options are more limited, so we can build something similar with two or three funds.

How this fits with the split between cash, bonds and stocks from lesson 9: the cash is your cushion, which you already built separately. Portfolios A and B are the stock part, for money you will not touch for 10 years or more. C adds bonds to soften the falls. D is for money you will use soon.

A. Two funds, the simple start

The easiest way to begin. In Argentina, both are available as CEDEARs.

  • 70% SPY
  • 30% QQQ

SPY tracks the S&P 500, the 500 largest companies in the US. It is the solid base. QQQ follows the Nasdaq-100, the 100 largest companies on the Nasdaq, without banks and with a heavy weight in technology. It earns more in good years and falls more in bad ones, which is why it gets a smaller share.

B. With an international broker

If you have access to more funds.

  • 40% SPY
  • 30% QQQ
  • 30% SCHD or VOOV

A third, more stable fund takes the place of part of SPY. SCHD groups companies that pay dividends consistently. VOOV groups the "value" companies in the S&P 500, more mature and cheaper. Both tend to fall less when technology falls.

C. The calmer one

If a 30% drop would make you sell.

  • 50% SPY
  • 20% QQQ
  • 30% short-term bonds

Portfolios A and B are 100% US stocks, in different flavors. This one adds short-term bonds (US Treasuries or bonds from good companies). It earns less over the long run, but when stocks fall, that part stays still and lets you sleep.

D. For a goal 1 to 3 years away

The trip, the car, the down payment on an apartment.

  • 100% low risk

Term deposits, money market funds, short-term bills or corporate bonds that mature before the date you need the money. If you are going to use it soon, it does not go into stocks: there is no time to recover from a fall.

How far can it fall? In 2008 the S&P 500 fell 37% over the year. In 2022 QQQ lost about a third. If you see that and keep buying, these portfolios work. If you see that and sell, none of them do.

What about individual stocks?

Other stocks you can consider for the long term are brands you know will not disappear in the coming years. Some examples (in Argentina, all available as CEDEARs): Berkshire Hathaway (BRK.B), Procter & Gamble (PG), Costco (COST), PepsiCo (PEP), Walmart (WMT), Coca-Cola (KO), Caterpillar (CAT) and McDonald's (MCD).

Two warnings. Kodak also seemed eternal, and it went bankrupt in 2012. General Electric was the most valuable company in the world, and in 2018 it was removed from the Dow Jones index. So if you add individual stocks, keep them a small portion of the investment fund (for example, up to 10 or 20%) and spread across several.

8. Review and rebalance

Twice a year, go back to your recipe.

The basics: lesson 9, Build your portfolio.

Over time, whatever rises the most takes up more room than you gave it. Rebalancing means bringing the portfolio back to its original recipe. Check it every 6 months or once a year, depending on how much it has moved.

How it drifted 65 / 35

You started with 70% SPY and 30% QQQ. Technology had a great year and now, in a portfolio of 10,000 dollars, there are 6,500 in SPY and 3,500 in QQQ.

How to fix it 70 / 30

You sell 500 dollars of QQQ and buy 500 of SPY. Even better: you sell nothing and for a few months put all new money into SPY until it is back to the recipe.

A practical rule: if any part has drifted more than 5 points from its target, rebalance. If it drifted less, leave it. Rebalancing forces you to sell a little of what went up and buy what fell behind, exactly the opposite of what your emotions ask for.

9. Other goals

You can save for the trip and the car too.

The basics: lesson 11, Age matters.

If you are saving for something other than retirement, like a trip or a car, it works the same way but with a different destination: conservative options like short-term bonds, corporate bonds or low-volatility mutual funds (portfolio D). That way you build up the money and the interest helps you get there.

It is not magic. What makes it work is paying yourself first: setting aside that 15 or 20% at the start of every month. If you have several goals, keep them in separate accounts or funds, so you do not spend the cushion on the trip without noticing.

12. Further reading

Six books, from easiest to hardest.

  1. The Richest Man in Babylon, George S. Clason. Short and told as stories. This is where "pay yourself first" comes from.
  2. The Psychology of Money, Morgan Housel. Why investing is more about behavior than math.
  3. The Simple Path to Wealth, J. L. Collins. Letters to his daughter about index funds and financial freedom.
  4. The Little Book of Common Sense Investing, John C. Bogle. The creator of the first index fund for ordinary investors explains why the index wins.
  5. A Random Walk Down Wall Street, Burton Malkiel. The history of bubbles and the evidence for not trying to beat the market.
  6. The Intelligent Investor, Benjamin Graham. The classic. Read chapters 8 and 20: Mr. Market and the margin of safety.

And if you want the basics at a calm pace, from inflation to how to split your money, there are the twelve lessons of Investing from Zero.

And when you want the next step (risk, cycles, factors, how to value a company without fooling yourself), there are the school readings.

It is not magic. It is paying yourself first, every month.
