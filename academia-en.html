Level 101

The fundamentals almost nobody explains.

101 · Source: Howard Marks

Risk is not the price moving

For Howard Marks, real risk is the chance of losing money for good, not how much the price goes up and down.

A lot of people measure risk by looking at how much a price shakes around. That shaking is called volatility. Howard Marks, an American investor famous for the letters he writes to his clients, says this mixes things up. For him, risk is the probability of a permanent loss: money that leaves and does not come back.

Look at two real cases. In 2020, during the pandemic, the S&P 500 index (about 500 of the biggest companies in the United States) fell close to a third in a few weeks. Anyone who did not sell was back at the high about six months after the fall started. That was volatility. On the other hand, anyone who owned shares of Lehman Brothers, a bank that went bankrupt in 2008, lost almost everything and never got it back. That was risk.

Permanent loss usually comes from three places: a business that breaks, paying an absurd price, or having to sell at the worst moment because you needed the money. And Marks adds something strange: risk is highest exactly when it feels lowest, because when everything is calm people get overconfident and pay any price.

Volatility is the entry fee for earning more than a certificate of deposit (CD). Permanent loss is what you have to avoid.

Try it: pick a well-known index or company, find its chart for the last 20 years and mark its worst fall. Then answer in writing: did it recover? If it recovered, it was volatility. If not, what broke to make it a permanent loss?

101 · Source: Benjamin Graham and Warren Buffett

Price, value and margin of safety

Price is what you pay, value is what you get, and the margin of safety is the gap between them that protects you when you are wrong.

The price is what the market asks for today. The value is what something is really worth, based on the money it will make over its life. Warren Buffett summed it up like this: price is what you pay, value is what you get. They are almost always close, but sometimes fear or euphoria pulls them far apart.

Benjamin Graham, Buffett's teacher, explained it with a character: Mr. Market, a moody partner who offers every day to buy your share or sell you his. Some days he is euphoric and asks too much, other days he is depressed and almost gives it away. You do not have to listen to him. You can wait.

Since nobody can calculate value exactly, Graham proposed the margin of safety: buy only when the price is well below what you think it is worth. If you think something is worth $100 and you pay $60, you have a 40% cushion. If you were wrong and it was really worth $80, you still paid less than it is worth. If you were right and the price reaches $100, you made $40 on $60, which is about 67%.

Notice that the margin does not come from a good company, it comes from the price. An excellent company bought at a crazy price can be a bad investment.

Try it: think of something used that you know well, like a phone or a bike. Estimate what it is really worth and find three listings for sale. For each one, calculate the margin: (value minus price) divided by value. Before you look, decide at what price you would say no.

101 · Source: Nassim Taleb

First, survive

Before trying to win big, rule number one is to stay in the game.

Nassim Taleb, a former market trader who wrote several books about chance and rare events, keeps coming back to one idea: some losses you can recover from and some losses knock you out of the game. The second kind is called ruin. If you are ruined, it does not matter how well you did before, because starting from zero, compound interest has nothing to work with.

The math is cruel because falls and rises do not weigh the same. If you have $100 and lose 50%, you have $50 left. To get back to $100 you need to make $50 on $50, which is +100%. If you lose 20%, +25% is enough ($20 on $80). If you lose 90%, you have $10 left and need +900%. And if you lose 100%, no rise can save you: any percentage of zero is zero.

That is why going up 50% and then down 50% does not leave you even: $100 becomes $150 and then $75. You lost a quarter.

How do people end up ruined? Almost always the same ways: putting everything into one thing, investing with borrowed money, or having to sell in the middle of a crash because there was no cushion. Taleb's idea is simple: make sure the worst thing that can happen to you is something you can handle.

Try it: write down the biggest fall you could handle without selling or changing your life, as a percentage. Then work out how much it would have to rise for you to recover: what you lose divided by what you have left. For example, if you lose 30%, you have 70% left, and 30 divided by 70 is about 43%.

101 · Source: Howard Marks

The market pendulum

Markets swing back and forth between euphoria and panic, and they almost never sit still in the middle.

Howard Marks compares the market to a pendulum. At one end is euphoria: "everything is going well, nothing can go wrong". At the other end is panic: "everything is going badly, nothing can go right". The pendulum spends very little time in the middle. It is almost always on its way to one of the ends.

The engine is not just the economy, it is psychology. When prices go up, people get excited, take less care and lend more easily, and that money pushes prices even higher. When prices fall, the same thing happens in reverse. One example: the Nasdaq index, full of tech companies, hit a high in 2000, right in the middle of the internet craze, and took about 15 years to get back to that level.

Marks says we cannot predict, but we can prepare. Instead of guessing the future, he suggests checking where we are with three questions: How much optimism is there? How afraid of losing are people? How easy is it to borrow money? Lots of optimism, little fear and easy credit are warning signs. The opposite often hides opportunities.

An honest warning: being early looks a lot like being wrong. The pendulum can keep rising for years after it starts to seem overdone to you.

Try it: this week, collect three signals: the day's economic headlines, what people are saying about investing in your group chats or social media, and how many easy loan offers show up for you. With that, place the pendulum from 1 (panic) to 10 (euphoria), write it down with the date and repeat the exercise in three months.

Level 201

Building and keeping a portfolio.

201 · Source: Vanguard (Jaconetti, Kinniry and Zilbering, 2010) and Larry Swedroe

Rebalancing with bands

Rebalancing is not a calendar chore: it is a rule that only kicks in when something has really drifted.

The 5-point rule has a problem with small pieces: if something is 10% of your recipe, drifting 5 points means ending up at half or one and a half times what you wanted. That is why many people use the 5/25 rule, made popular by the author Larry Swedroe: you rebalance when a piece drifts 5 points or 25% of its own weight, whichever comes first. For a 10% piece, the band goes from 7.5% to 12.5%. For a 70% piece, it is still 65% to 75%.

Inside the band, you do nothing. Every sale costs fees, the gap between the buying and selling price, and sometimes taxes. It is worth paying them to fix a real drift, not to chase noise. A 2010 Vanguard study did not find an ideal frequency: checking once or twice a year, with bands, is enough for most people.

The cheapest option is still not selling. With 10,000 dollars in 75% stocks and 25% bonds, and a 70/30 recipe, a 1,000 contribution sends 800 to bonds and 200 to stocks: you end up with 7,700 and 3,300, exactly 70/30.

And know when not to rebalance. The rule asks you to buy what fell, and that makes sense with a fund that buys a whole market. With a single stock, first ask yourself whether the company changed or only the price. If the business broke, buying more to hit a percentage is throwing good money after bad. And the cushion is never used for rebalancing.

Try it: write your recipe with a band for each piece (5 points or 25% of its weight, whichever is smaller) and write down where your next contribution goes if a piece moves outside its band.

201 · Source: Vanguard, "Dollar-cost averaging just means taking risk later" (2012)

All at once or bit by bit?

With a large sum, investing it all at once usually wins, but going in bit by bit on fixed dates protects you from yourself.

First, let's separate two things. Investing part of your paycheck every month is not a decision: it is investing the money when you have it. The real question shows up when you already have a large sum available, like a bonus, an inheritance or years of savings put together. Do you put it in today or spread it over several months?

Vanguard studied that question in 2012 with data from the US, the United Kingdom and Australia. It compared investing everything at once against spreading it over 12 months, and putting it all in at once won about two out of three times. The reason is simple: markets go up in more years than they go down, and money waiting in cash is not working.

So does averaging in not work? It works for something else. The times when spreading it out wins are mostly when the market falls right after you went in, and that is the scene that scares people the most. If putting it all in today will leave you unable to sleep and selling at the first drop, a bit-by-bit plan you stick to beats a perfect plan you abandon.

The key is that it has to be a plan and not an "I will wait for it to drop". Fixed dates, fixed amounts and a short timeline, for example 3 to 6 months. If you decide again every month, you are not averaging in anymore: you are trying to guess the market.

Try it: imagine you get 6,000 dollars today. Write down on paper what you would do: all at once, or six buys of 1,000 with the exact dates. If you choose bit by bit, schedule the buys today.

201 · Source: academic research (Fama and French, Jegadeesh and Titman, Novy-Marx and others)

Factors: what a stock is made of

A factor is a trait that many stocks share and that explains why they move together.

Every stock has traits: how cheap it is, how much it went up lately, how much it earns, how big it is, how much its price shakes around (that is volatility). Researchers found that a handful of those traits explains a good part of how stocks move, and that some of them paid a reward over long periods.

Value: companies that are cheap compared with what they earn or what they own. Over the long run they did better, but in the US they fell behind for much of 2007 to 2020. Momentum: what went up the most in recent months tends to keep going up for a while, until it suddenly turns around, like in 2009. Quality: companies that are profitable, stable and carry little debt. Size: small companies did better historically, although today people debate whether that reward is still alive. Low volatility: calm stocks that returned about the same as the jumpy ones, with fewer scares.

No factor pays every year. Each one has had long bad streaks. If you cannot stick with it at its worst moment, you will sell it right then.

And a factor fund holds whatever its rule picked last time. An S&P 500 momentum fund picks its stocks again twice a year: if the winners were almost all tech companies, the fund looks like a tech fund, even if the name does not say so. Even a "value" fund can end up with tech giants among its biggest holdings. The name is the label. What you own is what is inside.

Try it: go to the page of a factor fund (value, momentum or quality), write down its 10 biggest holdings and its weight by sector, and compare them with those of an S&P 500 fund. How similar are they?

201 · Source: Irving Fisher (real rate) and Argentine monetary history

The currency you measure in

A return only means something if you know which currency you measure it in and how much prices went up in that currency.

A big number is not always a gain. If a certificate of deposit (CD) in pesos paid you 100% in a year but prices went up 120%, you have twice as many pesos and you can still buy less than before. What they paid you is the nominal return. What you gained in buying power is the real return: 2 divided by 2.2 is 0.91, so you lost about 9%.

The same thing happens with devaluation, which is when your currency loses value against another one. If your investments in pesos went up 50% but the dollar went from 1,000 to 1,600 pesos, in dollars you lost a bit more than 6%. In Argentina, after ten years of one peso per dollar, in 2002 the peso ended up worth less than a third of a dollar. The same bills, from one year to the next.

That is why it makes sense to measure in a strong currency, like the dollar or the euro, which loses value much more slowly. But the dollar has inflation too: to know whether you really gained, also subtract US inflation.

The underlying rule is to measure in the currency you are going to spend. If you are going to live in euros, your goal goes in euros. If you spend in pesos but big things, like a car or a house, are thought of in dollars, the dollar is your yardstick. The danger is not owning things in another currency: it is saving in one and spending in another without having thought it through.

Try it: find out how much you had saved a year ago and how much you have today. Convert both numbers to dollars with the exchange rate of each date and compare them. Did you grow in pesos, in dollars or in neither?

Level 301

Analyzing companies without fooling yourself.

301 · Source: Aswath Damodaran, with two P/E traps pointed out by David Galán

Valuing is telling a story with numbers

A good valuation brings together a story about the company with numbers that make it possible, and even the most famous number can fool you.

Aswath Damodaran says that a story without numbers is a fairy tale, and numbers without a story are an empty spreadsheet. First you tell what the company is and what it is going to become. Then you ask yourself how big sales, margins and profits would have to be for that story to come true, and whether that is believable.

The most popular shortcut is the P/E (price to earnings): it tells you how many years of today's earnings you pay for when you buy the stock. First trap: the P/E for the last twelve months can be inflated by earnings that will not repeat. In November 2026, Amazon traded at 21.4 times its past earnings, but at 25.7 times its expected earnings. Its earnings had jumped 74% compared with all of 2025, largely because the value of its stake in Anthropic went up, not because the business earned more. Taking out that gain, a rough estimate puts the past P/E above 30 times. The clue: if the future P/E is higher than the past one, the market expects earnings to go down.

Second trap: cheap compared with its own history is not cheap compared with interest rates. If the market trades at 17.8 times, each dollar invested gives you 1 divided by 17.8, which is 5.6% in earnings. The 10-year US Treasury bond paid 5.1%. The difference is half a point, when before, with similar multiples, it was 2.5 to 3 points. Same number, much less reward for the risk of owning stocks.

Try it: pick a company you know and look up its past P/E (trailing) and its future P/E (forward) on a stock quote site. Calculate 1 divided by the P/E and compare it with the 10-year bond rate. Which is higher?

301 · Source: Aswath Damodaran, Steve Eisman and Howard Marks

The huge market that fools you

A market being gigantic does not mean the companies fighting over it are going to make money.

Damodaran describes the trap like this: a huge market shows up (people call it the TAM, the total addressable market, the full size of the possible business) and every company assumes it will grab a big share. They all invest as if they were going to win. They cannot all win. More money comes in than the business can pay back, price wars arrive and margins shrink. "The biggest market I have ever seen" is an alarm, not a reason to buy.

How the party gets paid for matters too. The dot-com bubble was paid for mostly with stock, so the crash wiped out wealth on the stock market and stopped there. The building of data centers for artificial intelligence is being paid for largely with debt. If it goes wrong, it is not just stocks that fall: loans can break and the hit reaches credit and the real economy.

Steve Eisman points out three places where risk hides. Concentrated customers: sales that look spread out but depend on very few buyers. Off-balance-sheet vehicles: debts set up in separate companies so the company looks less indebted. Gains from valuation: if a company owns shares of a customer and that customer goes up in value, the gain shows up in its results even though it did not sell anything. Howard Marks cited FactSet data: Alphabet and Amazon added up to about $151 billion in gains from investments in the second quarter of 2026.

Try it: take a company that talks a lot about its "huge market" and look for three things in its latest report: what share of sales comes from its biggest customers, how much debt and how many long-term leases it has, and whether part of its profit came from investments instead of the business.

301 · Source: Mohnish Pabrai and Howard Marks

Low risk is not the same as low uncertainty

Not being able to guess next year's earnings does not mean you could lose your money for good.

Uncertainty is not knowing how much the company will earn next quarter. Risk is the chance of losing your capital for good. Mohnish Pabrai says the market mixes the two up. It overpays for companies with smooth earnings and underpays for companies with jumpy earnings, even if they have lots of cash, no debt and a lasting advantage. Since many fund managers are judged quarter by quarter and cannot stomach those jumps, the discount sticks around.

An example Pabrai tells: a steel pipe maker, bought at about 40 to 42 dollars per share when it had about 15 dollars per share in cash and zero debt. Its earnings were impossible to predict, but losing everything was very unlikely. It ended up bought by another company at 160 dollars per share.

The idea breaks if the company spends its cushion on a bad acquisition, on buybacks paid with debt or on giant investments. That is why you always watch the balance sheet, not only when you buy. Howard Marks adds a test: can you estimate the earnings per share (total earnings divided by the number of shares) five years from now within a margin of 50% up or down? If you estimate 10 dollars, the real result should land between 5 and 15. If you can, the uncertainty has limits. If not, you are buying a story.

Try it: pick a company, look up its earnings per share for the last five years and its current cash and debt. Write down your estimate for five years from now and ask yourself if you would bet that it lands between half and one and a half times your number.

301 · Source: Steve Eisman

Cheaper is not always safer

If the reason you bought stopped being true, the stock being cheaper does not protect you.

The margin of safety means buying well below what something is worth, so you have a cushion if you are wrong. Steve Eisman tells about a mistake of his own: he held Citigroup shares long after his central idea, that the bank's profitability would get back to the level of its competitors, had already failed in plain sight. Every drop looked like an opportunity to him. In reality the market was putting the right price on a broken business.

A thesis is a few concrete things that have to be true for the investment to work. Eisman calls the mistake thesis creep, the thesis that slides without you noticing. It happens when the price becomes the argument: "it fell 40%, it has to be worth more than this".

His fix is mechanical. Write down in advance the facts your thesis needs, and if they stop showing up, the thesis is broken, no matter what the price does. It only works if you write facts that can fail, like "sales of the new product grow less than a certain number for two quarters in a row". A sentence like "good company, it will come back" never fails, and that is where the problem hides. Careful: sometimes a great company goes through a temporary rough patch that from the outside looks just like a broken thesis. Telling the two apart is the real work.

Try it: for a company you own or like, write on paper three facts about the business, not about the price, that would make you change your mind. Each one with a number and a deadline. Keep it and reread it when its next quarterly report comes out.

301 · Source: Charlie Bilello, with a counterpoint from Mohnish Pabrai

Knowing a company is not having an edge

Knowing a lot about a company does not tell you whether its price already reflects everything you know.

We tend to invest in what we know: the company we work for, the brands we use. That comfort feels like conviction. Charlie Bilello shows the risk with Nike, a brand half the world knows: over five years it fell more than 70% at one point while the S&P 500 went up. And according to his data, of the ten biggest companies in the United States in 1990, none is still among the top ten today, and from the 2000 list only Microsoft survived.

But concentrating is not always bad. Investors like Mohnish Pabrai bet big on purpose when they know something the market has not priced in yet. What separates one thing from the other is three questions. Do you know something the market does not, and do you keep it up to date? Is your exit rule about the business or only about a price? Does your paycheck depend on the same company? If you work there and also have a lot invested, a bad year hits you twice, at work and in your savings.

Informed concentration is betting big with a written thesis, facts that would prove it wrong and a size decided in advance. Familiarity bias is betting big because you like the brand. Knowing the product is not evidence. Having information is.

Try it: make a list of the companies you own or would like to own and write next to each one why. If the reason is "I use it" or "I know it", write down what you know that the market does not. If you cannot find anything, you already learned something.

Level 401

The big decisions.

401 · Source: Nassim Nicholas Taleb, Jeff Bezos

Can it be undone?

A mistake you can fix costs you money. One you cannot take back can knock you out of the game.

Not all mistakes weigh the same. If you buy a stock and you are wrong, you sell, lose something and move on. It hurts, but your money is still there, smaller, and it can grow again. Other mistakes cannot be fixed: putting all your savings into a business that closes, cosigning a loan you later cannot pay, lending a relative money that never comes back and that costs you the relationship on top of it.

Nassim Taleb keeps pushing a simple idea: the first thing is to stay in the game. If you lose everything, no future gain can save you, because you no longer have anything to keep going with. Jeff Bezos, in his 2015 letter to Amazon shareholders, explains it with doors. Some decisions are two-way doors: if you do not like what is on the other side, you come back. Others are one-way doors.

The rule is to treat them differently. With two-way doors, go for it, try things and make cheap mistakes. With one-way doors, go slowly even if the opportunity looks amazing. Before something big, like buying a house with a 20-year mortgage, the first question is not how much you could make. It is whether it can be undone.

Try it: write down the three biggest money decisions you have ahead of you this year. Next to each one write "two-way" or "one-way". For the one-way ones, write what would happen in the worst case and whether there is a way to test it small first, like renting in a neighborhood before buying there.

401 · Source: Emily Haisley (BlackRock), Gary Klein

Rules, not willpower

Your biases do not go away because you know they exist. You hold them back with written rules and with someone who checks you.

Knowing that fear, greed or pride can work against you does not make you immune. Emily Haisley, a behavioral finance researcher at BlackRock, puts it this way: biases are not a lack of information, they come built in. So instead of promising yourself to be more rational, it makes sense to set up your surroundings so the bias has less room.

Three simple tools. First, a checklist you fill out before any important buy or sale: Why am I doing this? What would have to happen for me to realize I was wrong? Am I calm, or am I deciding out of anger, fear or rush? Second, a devil's advocate: someone you trust who gains nothing from your decision and whose job is to find the hole in it. Have them give their opinion before hearing yours, so they do not just go along with what you think. Third, the premortem, an idea from the psychologist Gary Klein: imagine a year has passed, the decision turned out horribly, and write down why.

One last signal: if you catch yourself looking for someone to tell you yes, you probably already know the answer.

Try it: write your checklist with five questions and save it on your phone, next to the app you invest with. Then choose your devil's advocate and ask them, next time, to ask you those questions out loud before you confirm.
